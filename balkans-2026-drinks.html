Omiš to Salzburg. Native grapes, monastery beer, and what you're allowed to carry home. Venues verified against Google Places on 11 Aug 2026.

Read this first — you are driving

Montenegro and Bosnia both run a blood alcohol limit around 0.03%, roughly half the EU norm and low enough that a single glass of Vranac can put you over. Croatia, Slovenia, Austria and Germany sit at 0.05%, with 0.0 for novice and professional drivers.

I'm working from memory here, not a live lookup, and these limits get changed. The only safe operating rule on this trip is that the driver drinks nothing. Split it: one of you tastes at the winery, the other drives. Swap the next day.

Roadside checks in Montenegro and Bosnia are routine, not rare, and penalties for foreign drivers are handled on the spot. This is not the place to be slightly over.

Foundation

Six grapes that explain everything you'll drink

The Balkans kept its native varieties while most of Europe replanted with French ones. That's the whole reason this leg is interesting rather than just cheap.

Plavac Mali — and the Zinfandel bombshell

The dominant red of Dalmatia. Small berries, high alcohol, dark fruit, grippy tannin, thrives on white karst where nothing else will grow.

The interesting part is its parentage. Croatian winemakers had claimed for years that Zinfandel was originally Croatian, and DNA work at UC Davis in the early 2000s largely proved them right — the ancient Dalmatian variety Crljenak Kaštelanski (also called Tribidrag) turned out to be genetically identical to Californian Zinfandel and Italian Primitivo. Plavac Mali is a descendant of that grape, crossed with another old Dalmatian variety, Dobričić.

So when you drink Plavac Mali on Pelješac you're drinking Zinfandel's child, on the ground where Zinfandel came from. That's a genuinely good bar fact and it happens to be true.

Croatia · red

Pošip and Grk — the Dalmatian whites

Pošip is from Korčula, full-bodied, citrus and stone fruit, the serious Croatian white. Grk is the oddity: it grows almost exclusively in the sands around Lumbarda on Korčula, and its flowers are functionally female — it can't pollinate itself, so growers have to interplant Plavac Mali among the vines to get a crop at all. Rare, expensive, worth ordering once if you see it by the glass.

Croatia · white

Vranac and Krstač — Montenegro

Vranac — the name relates to the word for a black horse — is Montenegro's flagship red. Deep, robust, fairly tannic, better with food than without. Krstač is the native white, named for the cross shape the grape bunches form.

You'll see both everywhere under the Plantaže label, which dominates the country's production. A glass of Plantaže in Kotor old town runs around €4, which is good value by Adriatic standards.

Montenegro

Žilavka and Blatina — Herzegovina

The pair that make Herzegovina genuinely distinctive, and neither is grown seriously anywhere else.

Žilavka is the white — dry, mineral, apricot and almond notes, higher alcohol than you'd expect. It's grown on bare karst around Mostar, Čitluk and Ljubuški.

Blatina is the red, and it has the same problem as Grk: functionally female flowers, so it must be interplanted with a pollinator variety to set fruit at all. Growers here use Alicante Bouschet or Merlot. That agronomic quirk is why Blatina stayed local — it's a nuisance to plant.

Both are cheap relative to quality because Bosnia has almost no export wine reputation. This is the best value drinking of the whole trip.

Bosnia · both

Prošek — the name fight

Prošek is the traditional Dalmatian dessert wine made from grapes dried on straw mats — sweet, amber, nothing whatsoever to do with Prosecco except six shared letters. When Croatia applied to protect the name at EU level, Italy objected furiously on the grounds of confusion with Prosecco, and the application ran into serious trouble. I don't have a reliable read on where it finally landed, so don't quote me on the outcome. Drink it anyway if you see it — it's usually poured with dessert on the Dalmatian coast.

Detour 02 · The big one

Pelješac — Dingač and Postup

This is the single best wine decision available on the whole remaining route, and it sits right at Ston, which you're already stopping at. Potomje is roughly 40 minutes up the peninsula from Ston. Budget two and a half hours round trip with a tasting. Non-drinking driver required.

Why Dingač matters

Dingač is a strip of near-vertical south-facing slope on the seaward side of Pelješac, dropping to the Adriatic. The vines get sun three times — direct, reflected off the white karst, and reflected off the sea. Gradients reach around 45 degrees. Until relatively recently the grapes came out on donkeys.

It's generally cited as the first protected wine appellation in Croatia, designated in 1961. In the early 1970s growers cut a tunnel straight through the ridge from Potomje to reach the vineyards, which is still the way in and is worth driving through purely for the experience — single lane, unlit, rough-hewn.

Postup is the neighbouring appellation, same grape, slightly less extreme, usually a little cheaper.

The honest cost-benefit

Reviewers repeatedly make the same point: the flagship wines are available in Croatian supermarkets at similar or lower prices. If your goal is to buy Dingač cheaply, buy it at a Tommy or Konzum. Go to Potomje for the barrel tastings, the tunnel, the slope, and the small-batch bottles that never leave the cellar — not for a discount.

Stop 03 · Boka Bay

Kotor, and the two experiences worth booking

Kotor old town's wine bars are patchy — the two things genuinely worth your time here are both outside the walls, and both need arranging in advance.

Detour 03 · Routing-dependent

Šipčanik — a wine cellar in a bombed aircraft hangar

Only relevant if Day 11 routes you Žabljak → Podgorica → Trebinje rather than via Nikšić. If it does, this is a serious detour candidate.

Wine Cellar Šipčanik, 13. Jul Plantaže

4.8 · 263 reviews · daily 09:00–17:00

Karabuško polje, Tuzi — south-east of Podgorica

The cellar is a converted underground military aircraft hangar from a Yugoslav air force base, damaged during the 1999 NATO campaign and rebuilt as a wine cellar in the mid-2000s. It's a long, arched tunnel now lined with barrels — genuinely unlike anywhere else you'll drink on this trip.

It sits beside Ćemovsko polje, Plantaže's single contiguous vineyard block, which is frequently described as one of the largest single vineyards in Europe. Guided tours, tastings, and reviewers repeatedly praise the guides by name.

Sunday warning: a reviewer reports the shop was closed on a Sunday under Montenegrin law, so they could only buy at restaurant prices. If you want to carry bottles out, don't go on a Sunday.

How to do this leg properly

  • Email or phone ahead. Almost none of these have online booking. Walk-ins sometimes work and sometimes don't.
  • Cash, in KM. Assume no cards.
  • Watch the weekday trap. Domano is closed weekends; Andrija closes early Saturday and all Sunday; Nuić closes Sunday.
  • One winery, done properly, beats three rushed. If you only do one, Marijanović.

Stop 07 · Sarajevo

A brewery that kept a city alive

Sarajevska pivara — and why it matters

Brewery: 4.4 (544) · Pivnica HS beer hall: 4.4 (3,844) · Franjevačka 15

Founded in 1864, the brewery sits on a natural spring. During the 1992–95 siege, when the city's water system had failed, that spring became one of Sarajevo's main sources of drinking water — people queued for it under fire. Reviewers still mention it unprompted. Treat the building with that in mind; it isn't just a beer hall.

Pivnica HS is the attached hall — dark wood panelling, cool in summer, beers brewed on site including a blonde, a dark, an unfiltered and a premium. Live music Friday and Saturday. Closed Sundays. Food gets mixed reviews and one serious complaint about meat; several reviewers suggest sticking to the beer.

Best value trick: there's a brewery shop at Iševića sokak 29 (Mon–Sat, closed Sun) selling everything the brewery makes at a fraction of bar prices.

A note on drinking in Baščaršija

Sarajevo is a majority-Muslim city and alcohol is widely and unremarkably available — but not everywhere. Plenty of cafés in the old quarter serve coffee and no alcohol, and that isn't a mistake or a shortage. Check the menu rather than asking loudly.

Stop 08 · Salzburg

One night, and one obvious answer

Augustiner Bräu Mülln

4.5 · 5,611 reviews · Mon–Fri 15:00–23:00, Sat–Sun 14:30–23:00

Lindhofstraße 7, Salzburg

Founded by Augustinian monks in 1621 and still brewing. It is the single best evening on the return leg and it is not close.

How it works, because it confuses everyone:

  • Pay at the till first and take your receipt.
  • Take a stone mug from the shelf — half litre or full litre.
  • Rinse it yourself at the stone fountain. This is not optional and it's not decorative.
  • Hand the receipt to the tapper, who fills it from a wooden barrel.
  • Food comes from stalls along the corridor — roast pork, sausage, pretzels, cold plates, bread. You can also bring your own food in, which is a genuine tradition here, not a loophole.
  • Seating is communal and outdoors under chestnut trees. Nobody reserves.

It opens at 15:00, so this is an evening plan. Given Day 16 is a 1,000 km push, one stein and an early night is the sane version.

Stiegl-Brauwelt

4.4 · 4,350 reviews · daily from 11:30

Bräuhausstraße 9, Salzburg

Stiegl is Salzburg's own brewery, with records going back to 1492, and remains family-owned while most Austrian brewers have been absorbed by multinationals. The Brauwelt is a full visitor centre — multi-floor museum, film, guided tours in English, generous tastings, big shop, beer garden and restaurant. Free entry with a Salzburg Card.

Reviewers note that on Sundays you won't see production running. If you're doing a tour, weekday.

Austrian wine, honestly

Salzburg is not a wine region — Austria's vineyards are all in the east, in Wachau, Burgenland and Styria, hundreds of kilometres away. You can drink Austrian wine here but you're drinking imports from the other side of the country, which is a strange note to end a trip on after Pelješac and Herzegovina.

If you do want a glass: Grüner Veltliner is the signature white, peppery and dry. Zweigelt and Blaufränkisch are the reds. And ask for a Radler if you want something light — beer and lemonade, and the honest choice for whoever's driving in the morning.

The spirit

Rakija — a field guide

Rakija is fruit brandy, it is homemade almost everywhere, and it is offered constantly. Refusing is fine; refusing badly is not. Take a small one, say živjeli, sip it.

Etiquette

  • Živjeli ("to life") works across Croatia, Bosnia and Montenegro. Make eye contact.
  • It's frequently served as a welcome, before food, not after. Don't treat it as a digestif.
  • Homemade rakija is a point of family pride. If someone brings out an unlabelled bottle, that's a compliment.
  • Strength is commonly 40% and can be well north of that. There's no reliable way to know.
  • Best places to try it deliberately: Klub Invalida in Kotor, Rakija & Wine Bar in Budva, and any Herzegovina winery — Marijanović's walnut liqueur gets named repeatedly in reviews.

Logistics

Getting it back to Rotterdam

The border that actually matters

Croatia, Slovenia, Austria and Germany are in the EU. Montenegro and Bosnia are not. So the wine you buy in Herzegovina and Montenegro is coming into the EU when you cross back — and personal allowances apply.

The standard EU traveller allowance per adult, arriving from outside the EU, is:

  • 4 litres of still wine
  • 16 litres of beer
  • 1 litre of spirits over 22% ABV, or 2 litres of fortified/sparkling wine under 22%

Two adults doubles that. So realistically: eight bottles of Blatina and two bottles of rakija between you, and you're at the line. That is much less than you'll want after Herzegovina.

These allowances have been stable for years but I haven't verified them today — if you're planning to load the boot, check before you commit money.

Buy the Croatian wine last

The workaround

Wine bought inside the EU and moved within it for personal use is not subject to the same limits. So if you want volume, buy Pelješac wine on the way home rather than Herzegovina wine — but Pelješac comes before Bosnia on your route, and you'd be carrying it through two non-EU countries and back in.

Cleanest solution given your actual route: keep the Bosnian and Montenegrin purchases within allowance and treat them as the good bottles. Then top up in Austria on Day 15 if you want volume — that leg is EU-internal all the way home.

Heat

You are in August, driving a car that will sit in full sun at trailheads for hours. A boot in Montenegro in August will comfortably exceed 50°C, and that will cook wine — corks push, and the wine is flat by the time you open it in Rotterdam.

  • Buy on the last day of each leg, not the first.
  • Keep bottles in the cabin footwell with the aircon on, not the boot.
  • If you're leaving the car for a long hike, take the bottles out or accept the loss.
  • Rakija and fortified wine survive heat far better than table wine. If you want something to definitely make it home intact, buy spirits.
