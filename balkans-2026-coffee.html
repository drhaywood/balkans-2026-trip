Context

You are driving across three coffee empires in nine days

This is the single most useful thing to understand about the rest of the trip. The coffee changes because the empire changed, and the border where it changes is still visible in the cup.

1. The Venetian coast — espresso

Dalmatia and the Bay of Kotor were Venetian territory for centuries. Kotor was under Venice from 1420 until the republic collapsed in 1797, and the coffee habit that stuck is Italian: small, fast, standing or sitting outside for a long time.

What matters practically: the default cup on the coast is a short espresso or a macchiato, and the bar is a social institution rather than a caffeine transaction. Nobody is rushing you off the table. A "kava" ordered without qualification gets you an espresso in Croatia; in Montenegro it may get you a Turkish-style one, so specify.

2. The Habsburg interior — Kaffeehaus

Slovenia, inland Croatia and Austria inherited the Viennese coffee house: a big room, marble tables, newspapers on wooden rods, a waiter in a waistcoat, a glass of water beside the cup, and an unwritten right to sit for three hours on one order. UNESCO added Viennese coffee house culture to its intangible cultural heritage list in 2011.

The water glass is not a courtesy add-on — it's structural. It signals you're welcome to stay.

3. The Ottoman frontier — the džezva

Coffee reached Europe through Ottoman territory, and Bosnia is the deepest surviving pocket of that culture anywhere on the continent. In Sarajevo and Mostar the default is still unfiltered coffee brewed in a long-handled copper pot and served on a tray with a sugar cube and a piece of rahat lokum.

Bosnians will tell you firmly that bosanska kafa is not Turkish coffee, and they have a real technical point — see the ritual section below. Don't call it Turkish in Sarajevo.

Where the line actually falls on your route

You cross the Ottoman line somewhere between Počitelj and Mostar. Everything from Brela to Kotor is Venetian espresso country with a thin modern specialty layer on top. Mostar and Sarajevo are Ottoman. Salzburg is Habsburg. The third-wave specialty scene sits on top of all three and largely ignores them.

Stop 01 · Base: Lokva Rogoznica

Brew it yourself

You've got three mornings at altitude with alpine starts. Buy beans in Kotor before you leave — Too Hot to Handle or CAVE both sell retail — because there is nothing to buy in Žabljak. Water boils cooler at 1,450 m, so if you're brewing filter, go slightly finer or extend contact time; the usual altitude correction is to accept a longer brew rather than fight it.

The ritual

How to drink bosanska kafa without getting it wrong

Why it isn't Turkish coffee

The distinction Bosnians insist on is a real one in the method. In the Bosnian preparation, water is brought to the boil in the džezva first, a small amount is poured off and reserved, the finely ground coffee is stirred into the remaining hot water and returned to the heat to raise the foam, and the reserved water is then poured back in to settle the grounds. Turkish preparation typically combines coffee, water and sugar cold from the start.

The practical result: Bosnian coffee is served unsweetened, with sugar handled separately by the drinker, and it arrives as a small set rather than a single cup.

What arrives

  • The džezva — copper pot, long handle, grounds settled at the bottom.
  • One or two fildžani — small handleless cups.
  • A dish of sugar cubes.
  • A piece of rahat lokum.
  • A glass of water.

The order of operations

  • Drink the water first. It clears the palate. This is not optional in the etiquette.
  • Pour slowly and stop before the grounds move. Don't stir the pot.
  • Sugar goes in your mouth, not the cup. The traditional move — na kocku — is to dip a corner of the sugar cube into the coffee, hold it on the tongue, and drink through it. Foreigners almost always stir the sugar in; it isn't wrong, it's just not the local way.
  • The lokum is for the end, not between sips.
  • Leave the last centimetre. That's grounds.
  • Take an hour. That's ćejf. Ordering it and drinking it in eight minutes misses the entire point of the thing.

Trade notes

Things worth looking at with New Jozi eyes

Four things worth studying

  • Fabrika's four-site model in Sarajevo. A city smaller than Rotterdam supporting four specialty sites all at 4.6+, differentiated by function rather than menu — one for sitting, one for grabbing, one small and local. That differentiation is the interesting part, not the count.
  • CAVE weighing shots in Montenegro. One reviewer reverse-engineered their entire recipe from watching. Visible scales are a marketing asset in a market where nobody else does it — and the review it generated is worth more than any ad.
  • Split's split hours. 4 Coffee closes at 13:00. Meštar at 15:00. Fyaka in Makarska shuts 14:30 and reopens 17:30. These are businesses that decided when their customers actually come and refused to staff the dead hours. Worth thinking about against your own trading pattern.
  • Non-smoking as a stated selling point. In Croatia, Montenegro and Bosnia, "non-smoking" appears in reviews as an explicit reason people chose the place — Fyaka and Utopia both get it named. A constraint you take for granted in NL is a differentiator here.

Practical, all countries

  • Cash. Too Hot to Handle (Kotor), Mazel Tov (Mostar) and Cafe bar VIVA (Ston) are cash-only per recent reviews. Bosnia needs KM; several places refuse euros outright.
  • Smoking. Indoors smoking is normal in Bosnia and Montenegro and common in Croatian bars. Sit outside if it bothers you, which in August you will anyway.
  • Oat milk. Patchy. Reliable at Kaffee-Alchemie, CAVE and most Split specialty shops. Absent in Žabljak. Kawa in Sarajevo has almond, not oat.
  • Sunday. Utopia (Split), 4 Coffee (Split), Meštar (Split), EJTIIZ (Sarajevo) and 220GRAD (Salzburg) all close or shorten. Check before you plan a Sunday morning around one.
  • Buy beans in Kotor. It's your last chance before the Durmitor stretch, and Salzburg is your only other retail-quality option.
