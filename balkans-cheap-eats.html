Street food, markets & camp cooking · 8–23 August 2026

Eating Cheap

Four nights with a camp stove, twelve with a car boot, and a region where the best food is frequently the cheapest. Bakeries, green markets, grill windows, what to buy in each country, and what to actually cook on two burners.

00Read once

Five habits that halve the bill

1

The bakery is the meal, not the snack. Every town on this route has a pekara open from 05:00 to midnight selling burek, cheese pastry, filled rolls, pizza slices and fresh bread. In Croatia and Bosnia a filled pastry is roughly the price of a coffee, and it is a full lunch. Two people can eat properly for the cost of one restaurant starter. This single habit is worth more than every other tip here combined.

2

Eat the cheap fish, not the expensive fish. Menus divide whole fish into classes, and sea bass and dentex are priced by the kilo at rates that surprise people. Sardines, mackerel, anchovies and girice (tiny whitebait, fried and eaten whole) sit at the bottom of the price list and are, in August, better eating — oily fish is at its peak in high summer. A plate of grilled sardines costs a fraction of a bream and is more purely Dalmatian.

3

Walk two streets inland. The price gradient in Dubrovnik, Kotor and Mostar is brutal and it operates over about 200 metres. In Mostar, reviewers report fifteen ćevapi with bread for around €3 five minutes from the bridge, and considerably more than that at the bridge itself. Same food, same city, same day.

4

Order the thing that was cooked this morning. Grills are priced per portion of meat. Braises, stews and stuffed vegetables are made in a batch at dawn and sold until they run out, which makes them structurally cheaper. In Bosnia this is a whole institution — the aščinica — and it is also the most authentic food in the country.

5

Buy drinks at a shop, not a terrace. Wine, beer and rakija at supermarket prices across Croatia, Montenegro and Bosnia are cheap enough that you can drink genuinely good local bottles every night. A bottle of decent Plavac Mali or Vranac from a supermarket costs less than two glasses of house wine on a waterfront. You are camping with a cool box — use it.

01Act on this first

The Sunday problem

Your four camping nights start on a Sunday, in two countries where the shops are shut on Sundays. This is the one thing on the whole list that will actually bite you.

The trap

Germany closes completely on Sundays. Not "some shops" — effectively all retail, by law. Slovenia largely does too. Checking the shops nearest your Kamna Gorica campsite: the Mercator on Kranjska cesta in Radovljica is closed Sunday. The local produce shop Domača trgovina is closed Sunday. The big SPAR on Gradnikova is listed as closed both Saturday and Sunday.

You drive Munich → Bled on Sunday 9 August and pitch your first camp that evening. If you have not shopped before you leave Munich, your options that night are a petrol station or a restaurant.

The fix

Shop in Munich on Saturday evening, 8 August, before the shops shut around 20:00. Buy the dry goods and the first two dinners. German supermarkets are cheaper than Slovenian ones anyway, and considerably cheaper than Croatian coastal ones.

If you miss it: main railway station supermarkets are the standard German exception and generally do open on Sundays — the REWE at München Hauptbahnhof is the usual answer. Verify before relying on it. Motorway services and petrol stations are also open but priced accordingly.

02Everywhere, every day

The bakery

The single cheapest good meal from Slovenia to Bosnia, open from before dawn until midnight, including Sundays when everything else is shut.

How it works

A pekara is not a patisserie. It is a counter with trays of hot savoury pastry, filled rolls, pizza slices and bread, turned over continuously all day. You point, they weigh or count, you pay cash. In Bosnia you order pastry by weight — "trista grama", 300 grams, is a generous portion for one.

Critically, bakeries stay open on Sundays when supermarkets do not. Pekara Panis in Omiš runs 05:00–midnight, seven days. This is your fallback for every closure problem on the trip.

What to order

Burek and its family Must try Cheapest hot meal

Hand-rolled filo coiled into a spiral and baked. Burek is meat, sirnica cheese, zeljanica spinach and cheese, krompiruša potato — that naming holds in Bosnia and blurs in Croatia. Ask for kiselo mlijeko or yoghurt with it; it is meant to be eaten together and it costs almost nothing extra.

Price signal — reviewers in Sarajevo report around 5 KM (roughly €2.50) for a portion at the big Baščaršija buregdžinicas. In Croatian bakeries a slice is in the same ballpark. This is a full lunch.

Kifle, pogačice and filled rolls Worth it

The breakfast end of the counter. Kifle are crescent rolls, plain or with cheese; pogača is a flat soft bread; filled versions come with cheese, ham or olives. Reviewers of Pekara Poričanin in Sarajevo describe a salted crusty kifla for under 2 KM. Buy these the night before for breakfast in the tent.

Somun and lepinja Worth it Camp essential

The soft flatbread that ćevapi are served in. Buy it fresh from a bakery to eat at camp with cheese, ajvar and tomatoes — it is the base of the cheapest good meal you can assemble anywhere in the region, and it costs pennies.

03Camp legs

Markets and supermarkets

Where to buy the local ingredients, what each chain is for, and the one thing every green market on this route does to foreigners.

Read this before any green market

The tržnica or pijaca is where the good produce is — and it is also where visitors reliably get overcharged, because stalls frequently display no prices. This is not paranoia; it is the single most common complaint in reviews of every market on your route, from Omiš to Kotor to Markale in Sarajevo. One reviewer reports paying €30 for a slice of watermelon, some grapes and a dragon fruit in Omiš.

Three habits fix it entirely:

  • Ask the price per kilo before anything goes in a bag. "Koliko po kili?" Point at the scale.
  • Carry small notes. Handing over a €50 for €4 of tomatoes invites creative change. Reviewers report folded notes given back short.
  • Buy the things markets are genuinely better at — fish, cheese, cured meat, seasonal fruit — and buy staples at the supermarket where everything is priced and scanned.

None of this means avoid them. The produce is better and the cheese and cured meat are frequently excellent. It means shop like a local rather than like a cruise passenger.

What to cook at camp

Six local dishes that need one pan, ingredients from the shops above, and no equipment you don't already have.

Slovenia — Kamna Gorica, 9–11 Aug

Kranjska klobasa with sauerkraut and potato

One pot · 25 min · Slovenia's protected sausage

Buy: Kranjska klobasa (Mercator, from the counter — the real one is pinned with a wooden skewer through the loop) · jar of sauerkraut (kislo zelje) · potatoes · one onion · bay leaf · caraway · mustard and horseradish · rye bread

  1. Cube the potatoes, cover with water, boil until nearly done.
  2. Add the drained sauerkraut, a sliced onion, a bay leaf and a good pinch of caraway. Simmer 10 minutes.
  3. Lay the sausages on top whole, lid on, 8–10 minutes. Do not prick them — the whole point of a protected Kranjska is that the fat stays inside.
  4. Serve with mustard, horseradish and dark bread.

This is genuinely how it is eaten here, not a camping compromise. Total cost is a few euros for two.

Žganci with ocvirki

One pot · 20 min · the Bohinj mountain staple

Buy: buckwheat flour (ajdova moka — Mercator, near the flours) · salt · pork cracklings (ocvirki, from the deli counter) or diced bacon · optionally sour milk or plain yoghurt

  1. Bring roughly a litre of well-salted water to the boil.
  2. Tip in about 300 g buckwheat flour all at once, in a mound in the centre. Do not stir. Boil 10 minutes.
  3. Poke a few holes down through the mound with a spoon handle so steam escapes. Boil 5 more minutes.
  4. Pour off most of the water into a mug, keeping it. Mash and beat the flour hard with a wooden spoon until it forms coarse crumbs, adding the reserved water a splash at a time.
  5. Fry the cracklings until they release their fat, and pour the whole lot — fat included — over the top.

Peasant food, mountain food, and about as Slovene as it gets. Eat with a glass of sour milk if you want the full version.

Dalmatia — Lokva Rogoznica, 11–13 Aug

Blitva na dalmatinski

One pot · 20 min · the dish every Dalmatian household eats

Buy: Swiss chard (blitva — market, sold in big bunches) · potatoes · several cloves of garlic · good olive oil · salt · lemon

  1. Cube the potatoes and boil in salted water for about 10 minutes.
  2. Wash the chard, chop stems and leaves roughly, add to the same pot. Cook 6–8 minutes until everything is soft.
  3. Drain, keeping a little of the water. Return to the pot off the heat.
  4. Add sliced raw garlic and a genuinely generous amount of olive oil — more than feels right — plus salt and lemon. Crush it all together roughly with a fork.

Served alongside grilled fish in every konoba on the coast, and eaten alone as a light supper in every house. The olive oil is the dish; do not be shy with it.

Dagnje na buzaru — mussels

One pan · 12 min · the fastest good meal on the coast

Buy: 1 kg mussels (Omiš fish market, before 14:00) · 4–5 garlic cloves · parsley · a glass of white wine · olive oil · dry breadcrumbs · optional tinned tomato for the red version · bread

  1. Scrub the mussels and pull off any beards. Discard any that are open and won't close when tapped.
  2. Warm olive oil, sliced garlic and chopped parsley stalks in a wide pan — do not brown the garlic.
  3. Tip in the mussels, pour over the wine, lid on, high heat. Shake the pan rather than stirring.
  4. After 4–5 minutes, when they are all open, stir through a heaped spoonful of breadcrumbs and the parsley leaves. The crumbs thicken the liquor into a sauce — this is the actual trick of buzara.
  5. Eat straight from the pan with bread. Use an empty shell as tongs, which is what locals do.

Add a tin of chopped tomatoes with the wine for crvena buzara. Works identically with clams or prawns.

Grilled sardines, in foil or a pan

10 min · the cheapest fish on the coast and the best in August

Buy: fresh sardines (ask the fishmonger to gut them — "očistite, molim") · coarse salt · olive oil · lemon · garlic and parsley

  1. Salt the sardines heavily inside and out and leave 15 minutes. Pat dry.
  2. On a campsite grill: straight on the bars, 2–3 minutes a side, until the skin blisters. On a stove: a dry hot pan, same timing. In foil: a double parcel with oil, garlic and lemon slices, 10 minutes.
  3. Dress with olive oil, lemon and chopped garlic-parsley the moment they come off.

Oily fish peak in high summer, which makes August exactly the right month for this and exactly the wrong month to pay four times as much for sea bass. Eat with blitva and bread.

The no-cook plate

Zero minutes · for arrival nights and long drives

Buy: somun or fresh bread (bakery) · Paški sir or another hard sheep cheese · Dalmatian pršut · ajvar · tomatoes · olives · figs · a bottle of Plavac Mali

  1. That's it. Cut things up.

Ajvar is the one to buy properly: roasted red peppers and aubergine cooked down to a thick relish, sold in jars everywhere from Slovenia south. Look for domaći (homemade) at a market rather than the supermarket version. It goes on everything for the rest of the trip.

05All twelve non-camping days

Street food, country by country

What to eat standing up. Prices are indicative — treat them as ratios rather than quotes.

Bavaria

Leberkässemmel Must try ~€3–4

A thick slab of griddled Leberkäse — a baked loaf of finely ground pork and beef, containing neither liver nor cheese — in a crusty roll with sweet or hot mustard. Sold at butcher counters and Viktualienmarkt stalls. The definitive cheap Bavarian lunch and excellent road food.

Brezn and Obatzda Worth it ~€5 together

A lye pretzel and a tub of the orange beer-garden cheese spread. Buy both at the market, take them into the self-service section of a beer garden, and buy only the beer — which you are legally entitled to do and locals routinely do.

Slovenia

Burek and bakery pastry Worth it

Slovenia is the northern edge of burek country and it is everywhere, generally cheaper than a sandwich. Also look for štruklji sold ready-made at markets and delis — the rolled curd-cheese dumplings, sold by the piece, warm.

Kremšnita, from a bakery not a hotel If time

The Bled cream cake is a fixed recipe, and a bakery version costs a fraction of a lakeside terrace one. If you want it with the view, pay for the view knowingly.

Croatia

Soparnik Must try Sold by the slice

Your local dish and it is street food, not restaurant food. Two paper-thin sheets of dough with chard, onion and parsley between, baked under hot ashes, brushed with garlic and oil, cut into diamonds and sold by weight. EU protected-designation, from Poljica — the district your campsite sits in. Look for it at markets, bakeries and roadside stalls between Omiš and Split.

Girice and fritule Must try

Girice — tiny whitebait, floured, deep fried, eaten whole with lemon. Sold cheap at any konoba and at fish counters. Fritule are small rum-and-citrus doughnuts sold from stalls in summer.

Ćevapi and pljeskavica in Croatia Worth it

Grill windows exist here too, and are noticeably cheaper than any konoba. Ordered in fives or tens with onion and ajvar. Good for a fast lunch in Omiš or Split.

Montenegro

Priganice Must try A few euro

Hot fried dough balls with honey, kajmak and cheese, sold from small places all over the Boka. Breakfast, dessert or an afternoon thing. The salty fresh dairy against raw honey on hot dough is the whole point.

Burek and pekara food, Kotor Must try

Kotor's old town restaurants are priced for cruise passengers. The bakeries three streets back are not. This is the biggest single price gap you will encounter in Montenegro.

Njeguški pršut, bought by weight Worth it

Buying a couple of hundred grams at a Njeguši producer costs a fraction of a restaurant charcuterie board and is better ham. Eat it at a picnic table on the Lovćen road with bread and cheese from the same shop.

Bosnia and Herzegovina — the cheapest country on the route

Ćevapi, away from the bridge Must try ~3 KM for 15

Reviewers at Ćevabdžinica Kod Bahre in Mostar report fifteen ćevapi in bread for around 3 KM — roughly €1.50. That is not a typo and it is five minutes from the tourist strip. Sarajevo's Bravadžiluk ćevabdžinicas are similar. This is the best value food anywhere on your route.

Aščinica trays Must try A few KM a portion

Point-and-choose home cooking: japrak, sogan-dolma, sarma, bosanski lonac, beans. Cooked at dawn, sold until gone, no grill, no English menu. Half the price of anywhere with a view and the most genuinely local meal in the country.

Bosnian coffee Must try ~3.50 KM

Reviewers at Caffe Divan in Sarajevo report about 3.50 KM — under €2 — for a full Bosnian coffee set with lokum, and being left alone for forty-five minutes. That is an hour of Sarajevo for the price of a bottle of water in Dubrovnik.

Sujuk, smoked cheese and dried meat Worth it

From the covered Markale hall. Reviewers describe vendors cutting free samples across the counter. Buy smoked fresh cheese and dried beef for the drive to Salzburg — this is the best car food on the trip and it travels.

Austria

A Bosna Must try ~€5

Salzburg's own street food, invented here in the late 1940s: grilled bratwurst in a crisp roll with onion, mustard, parsley and a heavy dusting of curry powder. Sold from small windows on and around the Getreidegasse. Named after the country you will have just spent four days in, for reasons nobody agrees on. Cheapest good thing in Salzburg.

Augustiner Bräu, self-service Worth it

Not street food but it behaves like it. You rinse your own stone mug, get it filled from a wooden barrel, and buy food from a market arcade of stalls rather than a kitchen — pork belly, pretzels, radishes, fish. Assembling a meal there costs a fraction of a restaurant dinner in the old town.

Drinking at shop prices

The gap between shop and terrace prices for wine in Croatia, Montenegro and Bosnia is wide enough to change how you travel. A decent supermarket bottle of Plavac Mali, Vranac or Žilavka costs roughly what a single glass costs on a waterfront. With a cool box and four camping nights, buying a case at the start of the Dalmatian leg is straightforwardly the right move.

Beer to try at shop prices: Union and Laško (Slovenia), Ožujsko and Karlovačko (Croatia), Nikšićko (Montenegro, brewed since 1896), Sarajevsko (Bosnia, 1864). None is remarkable; all are the correct thing to be drinking where you are.

And the free one: tap water is drinkable throughout Slovenia, Croatia, Montenegro and Bosnia, and in the mountains it is genuinely excellent. Refill bottles rather than buying them. Stretches of the Tara are drinkable straight from the river.
