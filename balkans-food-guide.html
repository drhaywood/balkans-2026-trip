Eating the Route

Ten cuisines along one road — Bavarian beer-cellar, Alpine Slovene, Venetian Dalmatian, Ragusan, Montenegrin coast and mountain, Ottoman Herzegovina, Sarajevo, and a prince-archbishop's kitchen. What to order, what it actually is, and where.

Six rules that travel the whole way

Ordering knowledge

Fish is sold by weight, and there are two classes. From Dalmatia to Montenegro, whole fish is priced per kilo, and menus divide it into categories — first class (sea bass, sea bream, dentex, John Dory) and lower classes (mackerel, sardine, horse mackerel). The waiter brings a tray of raw fish to the table; you pick one, they weigh it, and the bill is the weight times the per-kilo price. Always ask the price per kilo and ask to see the fish weighed. This is where a €25 dinner quietly becomes €70. It is not a scam, it is simply how it works — but only if you ask.

Counter-intuitively, the cheap classes are often the better eating in August. Grilled fresh sardines and mackerel off a charcoal grill are one of the great Adriatic pleasures and cost almost nothing.

Ordering knowledge

Anything cooked slowly must be ordered a day ahead. Peka in Croatia, ispod sača in Montenegro and Bosnia, boiled lamb in Durmitor — all take three to five hours over embers. Restaurants will not have it waiting. The move is to phone or drop in on your first day and book it for your second. Doing this on arrival in Brela and again in Žabljak buys you two of the best meals of the trip.

Ordering knowledge

Burek is meat. Only meat. In Bosnia the filled pastries are named by filling: burek (meat), sirnica (cheese), zeljanica (spinach and cheese), krompiruša (potato). Collectively they are pita. Asking for a "cheese burek" marks you instantly. In Croatia the word has drifted and burek can mean any of them — so the rule changes at the border. In a proper buregdžinica you order by weight: "trista grama bureka" — 300 grams — which is a generous single portion.

Ordering knowledge

Coffee is a unit of time, not a drink. Bosnian coffee arrives as a ritual: a copper džezva, a small handleless fildžan, a sugar cube and usually rahat lokum. Pour a little, let it settle, hold the sugar in your teeth and drink through it. It takes half an hour and that is the entire point. Do not order one if you are leaving in ten minutes. In Dalmatia and Montenegro, coffee is equally slow but Italian in form — a bijela kava or macchiato, drunk sitting down, for as long as you like. Nobody will bring the bill until you ask.

Ordering knowledge

Three words for the local hole-in-the-wall, and each means something specific. A konoba (Croatia, Montenegro) was originally the cellar where wine and cured meat were kept — it now means a small, family, wood-fire place. A gostilna (Slovenia) or gasthof/wirtshaus (Austria, Bavaria) is the traditional inn. An aščinica (Bosnia) is the one worth hunting: a home-cooking canteen serving pre-made stews and braises from trays, point-and-choose, no grill, no menu translation, half the price of anywhere with a view. It is the single most authentic meal in Bosnia.

Money

Croatia and Montenegro are euro. Bosnia is the convertible mark (KM/BAM), pegged at 1.95583. Euros are taken at a rounded, poor rate. Several of the best cheap places in Mostar and Sarajevo are cash only in KM — draw local currency on arrival. Tipping: round up or ~10% across the region; in Bavaria and Austria you say the total you want to pay as you hand over money rather than leaving coins on the table.

01Day 1–2 · 8–9 AugMunich · Bavaria

Munich

You arrive Saturday evening and leave Sunday morning. That happens to line up exactly with the one Bavarian meal that is strictly time-limited.

Dishes

Weißwurst Must try

The great Munich breakfast, and the most rule-bound food in Germany. A pale veal-and-pork sausage with parsley and lemon, poached not grilled, served floating in its own hot water in a tureen. Because it was historically made fresh each morning with no preservatives, tradition holds that Weißwurst must not hear the noon bells — eaten before midday, or not at all. Plenty of places now serve it later; ordering it at 6pm still marks you.

The eating is a technique. Either slice it lengthways and roll the meat out of the skin, or do it properly and zuzeln — bite the end and suck the sausage out. The casing is never eaten. It goes with sweet mustard (süßer Senf — never the sharp stuff), a Brezn, and a Weissbier. Beer with breakfast is not a joke here; it is the correct pairing.

Timing — Sunday morning, before you drive to Bled. This is the single most Bavarian thing available to you and your schedule hands it to you for free.

Obatzda Must try

The beer garden's own invention and a genuinely clever piece of thrift: over-ripe Camembert mashed with butter, sweet paprika, caraway, onion and a splash of beer, into an orange spread eaten with a pretzel and raw onion rings. It exists because ripe cheese had to be used before it turned, and it is credited to a beer-garden landlady in the 1920s. Salty, funky, and exactly right with a cold Helles.

Schweinshaxe Worth it

Roast pork knuckle: a fist of meat under lacquered crackling, with a potato dumpling and dark beer gravy. Enormous, and the crackling is the reason to order it. If you want the same animal in a lighter form, Schweinsbraten — roast pork shoulder with the same gravy — is the everyday version and half the fight.

Leberkäse Worth it

A baked loaf of finely ground pork and beef, sliced thick, griddled, and served in a bread roll with mustard — the Leberkässemmel. Contains neither liver nor cheese, despite the name. It is the great Bavarian snack and it costs almost nothing at a butcher's counter or a Viktualienmarkt stall. The best road food available before you cross the Alps.

Steckerlfisch and Radi If time

Two beer-garden things worth knowing. Steckerlfisch is a whole mackerel, brined and grilled over charcoal on a stick — smoky, oily, excellent. Radi is a large white radish cut in a continuous spiral and heavily salted until it weeps; it is salty specifically so that you drink more beer, which is the entire commercial logic of a beer garden in one vegetable.

Kaiserschmarrn Worth it

A thick pancake torn into pieces in the pan, caramelised with sugar and butter, dusted heavily and served with plum compote or apple sauce. Named for Emperor Franz Joseph. You will meet it again in Salzburg at the other end of the trip, so if you skip it here you have not lost it.

Drink

Helles, and specifically Augustiner Must try

Munich's own style is Helles — pale, malty, soft, made to be drunk by the litre rather than admired. Six breweries have the right to serve at Oktoberfest, and the local consensus favourite is Augustiner, Munich's oldest, founded 1328. Look for Edelstoff, the stronger export version. Served from wooden barrels where you're going, which changes it noticeably — softer, less carbonated.

Also here: Dunkel (the original Munich beer, dark and bready), Weissbier (wheat, cloudy, banana-clove yeast character), and Radler — half beer, half lemonade, credited to an innkeeper in 1922 who ran short of beer with a hundred cyclists arriving and cut it with lemonade. Genuinely the right drink at 32°C.

Know this — you are legally entitled to bring your own food into a Munich beer garden's self-service section. Locals turn up with bread, radishes and a tablecloth, and buy only the beer.

02Days 2–4 · 9–11 AugBled · Radovljica · Bohinj

Alpine Slovenia

A cuisine caught between Austria, Italy and the Balkans, which sounds like a compromise and is actually the appeal.

Where you actually are

Camping at Kamna Gorica puts you ten minutes from Radovljica and its old town, and Radovljica is materially better eating than lakeside Bled — less captive, less priced-for-the-view. Bohinj, forty minutes the other way, is better again for mountain food. Bled itself is where you go for one specific cake.

Dishes

Kremšnita Must try

The Bled cream cake, and one of very few desserts in Europe with a documented inventor and a fixed specification. Codified in 1953 by Ištvan Lukačević, then pastry chef at the Hotel Park: a layer of vanilla custard, a layer of whipped cream, puff pastry top and bottom, cut into a seven-centimetre square. That is the spec and it does not vary. The Park still claims the original and sells them by the thousand.

How to do it — on the lakeside terrace, with a coffee, after the Osojnica climb. It is a genuinely great cake and it is also completely unpretentious.

Potica Must try

The Slovene celebration cake: a very thin sheet of yeasted dough spread with filling and rolled into a spiral, so a slice is a dozen alternating rings. Walnut (orehova) is the classic. The one to look for is tarragon potica (pehtranova) — sweet dough filled with tarragon, sugar and cream. It sounds wrong, it is a genuinely Slovene thing, and it is superb: herbal, aniseed-ish, not remotely like a herb has wandered into a pudding.

Štruklji Must try

Rolled dumplings — thin dough wrapped around a filling, rolled into a log, then boiled or baked and sliced. There are said to be dozens of traditional fillings. The default is skutni, curd cheese, served savoury with breadcrumbs and butter alongside meat, or sweet with sugar. Tarragon appears here too. It is the dish that most clearly is not Austrian and not Italian — it is simply Slovene.

Kranjska klobasa Must try

Carniolan sausage, and one of the most legally protected sausages in Europe. The EU spec is strict: coarsely chopped pork and bacon, salt, pepper, garlic, water — nothing else, no more than 20% bacon, pinned with a wooden skewer through the loop. Boiled, served with mustard and horseradish and dark bread. This is the sausage that emigrated to Australia and became the "kransky", so you may have met a distant, sadder relative already.

Žganci Worth it

Buckwheat spoonbread — coarse porridge crumbled into lumps, dressed with ocvirki, pork cracklings and their fat. Peasant food, mountain food, and the Bohinj version is the reference. Order it as a side with something braised rather than as a main and it makes complete sense.

Prekmurska gibanica Worth it

Nothing to do with these mountains — it is from the far northeast — but it is on every traditional menu and it is EU-protected. Layered pastry with four fillings in strict order: poppy seed, cottage cheese, walnut, apple, repeated. Dense, ancient-feeling, and served in a slab. Share one.

Lake trout Worth it

Grilled whole with butter, parsley and potatoes, or cold-smoked as a starter. Slovenia farms and fishes trout seriously, and in Bohinj and the Radovljica valley it is the local protein rather than an import. Several of the best restaurants here build their menu around it.

Jota If time

Sauerkraut, beans, potato and smoked pork, cooked long. Excellent food, and very obviously winter food. In August it is a heavy lunch you will regret in the car. Worth knowing about; worth eating in November.

Drink

Orange wine from Goriška Brda Must try

The genuine foodie find in Slovenia. Skin-contact white wine — white grapes fermented on their skins like a red, producing an amber, tannic, oxidative, savoury wine — was revived in the 1990s in the hills on the Slovene-Italian border, and Goriška Brda is one of the two places on earth where the modern movement started. Look for Rebula (Ribolla Gialla) done this way. It is nothing like any other white wine and it happens to be superb with cured pork and hard cheese.

Elsewhere on the list: Teran from the Karst, a fierce, high-acid red grown on red iron soil; Šipon (Furmint) from the east; and Cviček, a pale, low-alcohol red blend that Slovenes drink like water and outsiders rarely understand.

Honey, and things made from it Worth it

Slovenia is disproportionately a beekeeping country — Anton Janša, born beside your campsite, was Maria Theresa's court beekeeper and World Bee Day is his birthday. The consequence on a menu is medica (honey brandy), honey liqueurs, and lect — hard decorated honey-dough hearts, made in Radovljica for generations. There is a working lect workshop and museum under one of the restaurants below.

03Days 4–6 · 11–13 AugLokva Rogoznica · Omiš · Brela

Dalmatia

Four hundred years of Venice, olive oil instead of butter, and a whole cuisine built on cooking things slowly under a metal bell.

Where you actually are

Autocamp Sirena is at Lokva Rogoznica, which sits between Omiš and Brela — about twenty minutes north of Punta Rata. That matters for eating: Omiš is closer than Brela, it is a working town rather than a resort strip, and it has the better konobas. It also puts you inside Poljica, the old peasant republic, which has its own protected dish.

Dishes

Peka Must try

The defining Dalmatian method and the meal to build a day around. Meat — usually veal, lamb or octopus — with potatoes, onion, olive oil, rosemary and white wine, placed in a shallow pan, covered with an iron bell (the peka), and buried in embers on the hearth for three hours or more. The bell traps the steam so nothing dries; the coals on top brown everything. The potatoes soak up the fat and are, quietly, the best part.

Octopus peka is the one to get if you want the version that is hardest to find at home. It goes gelatinous and sweet in a way grilled octopus never does.

How to do it — must be ordered 24 hours ahead, and usually for a minimum of two people. Book it on your first afternoon, eat it on your second night. Several places will collect you and drive you back for free, which solves the wine problem.

Soparnik Must try

Your local dish, and almost nobody outside Croatia has heard of it. Two paper-thin sheets of unleavened dough with a filling of Swiss chard, onion and parsley between them, baked directly on a stone hearth under hot ashes, then brushed with garlic and olive oil and cut into diamonds. It is EU protected-designation and it comes from Poljica — the district you are camped in. Vegan by accident, ancient by design, and completely unlike anything else on the coast.

Pašticada Must try

The great Dalmatian celebration dish and a two-day project. Beef topside is studded with garlic, bacon and cloves, marinated in vinegar and wine overnight, browned, then stewed for hours with prunes, dried figs and prošek dessert wine until the sauce is dark, glossy and sweet-sour. Served with gnocchi to catch it. If you see it on a menu, it is a house's signature and it will have been made properly.

Crni rižot Must try

Black risotto — cuttlefish or squid with their ink, garlic, wine and a little tomato. It stains everything, including you. Done well it is intensely of the sea and not remotely fishy; done badly it is grey and gluey. It is the single best test of whether a konoba's kitchen is serious.

Buzara Must try

Not a dish so much as a method: shellfish — mussels, clams, langoustines — flashed in olive oil, garlic, parsley, white wine and breadcrumbs. Bijela buzara is the white version; crvena adds tomato. The breadcrumbs are the trick, thickening the liquor into something you finish with bread. Order it, then order more bread.

Brudet Worth it

Fisherman's stew — several kinds of fish, tomato, vinegar, onion, cooked in one pot and never stirred with a spoon (you shake the pan, or the fish breaks). Served with soft polenta. It is what was made from whatever did not sell, and it is far better than that origin suggests.

Paški sir and Dalmatinski pršut Worth it

The standard opening plate, and both are properly good. Pag cheese is a hard sheep's milk cheese from an island so wind-blasted that salt spray coats the herbs the sheep graze on — the salinity is in the animal before it is in the cheese. Dalmatian prosciutto differs from Italian in being lightly smoked and then dried in the bura wind. Order both with olives and capers and a glass of something white.

Grilled sardines and small fish Worth it

The cheap end of the fish menu and, in August, frequently the better eating. Fresh sardines over charcoal with nothing but salt, oil and lemon; girice, tiny fried whitebait eaten whole. A plate of either costs a fraction of a sea bass and is more purely Dalmatian.

Fritule and rožata If time

Dessert here is thin. Fritule are small rum-and-citrus-zest doughnuts, usually festival food. Rožata is a Dubrovnik custard — see the next section, where it belongs.

Drink

Plavac Mali, and knowing what to ask for Must try

The great Dalmatian red. DNA testing eventually established it as a cross of Dobričić and Crljenak Kaštelanski — and Crljenak turned out to be Zinfandel, meaning California's signature grape is a Dalmatian native that had almost died out at home. Plavac Mali is its child: dark, high-alcohol, dried-fig and Mediterranean-herb.

Whites to ask for: Pošip (Korčula — full, nutty, the best white on the coast), Malvazija (Istrian, crisper), and if you ever see Grk, order it — it grows on one sandy strip of Korčula, has only female flowers so needs Plavac interplanted to pollinate, and barely leaves the island.

Don't confuse — prošek is a sweet Dalmatian dessert wine made from dried grapes. It is not prosecco, and Croatians are touchy about the confusion for good legal reasons.

Rakija, correctly Worth it

On the coast the base is grape — loza. The infusions are where it gets interesting: travarica (wild herbs, the Dalmatian default), orahovica (green walnuts, dark and bitter-sweet), medica (honey), smokovača (fig). Most konobas make their own and will bring you a shot at the end unbidden. Refusing it is worse manners than accepting it.

04Day 6 · Thu 13 AugSton · Mali Ston · Pelješac

Ston

A bay that has been farming the same oyster since the Romans, at the foot of five kilometres of wall built to protect salt.

Dishes

Mali Ston oysters Must try

These are Ostrea edulis, the native European flat oyster — not the Pacific rock oyster that makes up almost all of what is sold in Europe today. Flat oysters are slower-growing, far rarer, and taste completely different: metallic, mineral, almost coppery, with a long finish rather than a clean brine. The bay works because the Neretva's fresh water meets the sea here at exactly the right salinity, and they have been farmed on this spot since Roman times.

Honest note on August — the traditional peak is around St Joseph's Day, 19 March. Summer is spawning season for flat oysters, and a spawning oyster can be softer and milkier than one eaten in spring. They are still excellent and absolutely worth the stop; they are simply not at their theoretical best. Eat them raw with lemon and nothing else, and try one grilled for contrast.

Mussels na buzaru Worth it

Farmed alongside the oysters on the same ropes, and cheaper by a wide margin. A kilo of Ston mussels in white wine, garlic and breadcrumbs is arguably better value than the oysters and just as much a product of this bay.

Stonski makaruli Worth it

Ston's genuinely strange contribution: a sweet cake made with pasta in it. Macaroni is boiled, mixed with ground walnuts or almonds, sugar, eggs, cinnamon and chocolate, and baked. It has been made here since at least the sixteenth century and traditionally served at weddings. It should not work. It is not exactly dessert as you know it, and it is worth ordering purely because you will not meet it anywhere else.

Dingač and Postup Must try

You are on the peninsula that makes the best Plavac Mali in Croatia. Dingač was the country's first protected wine appellation, designated 1961; Postup is its neighbour. Both grow on absurdly steep south-facing slopes falling straight to the sea, where the vines get sun three ways — direct, reflected off the water, and radiated back off white limestone. Until 1973 the grapes came down by donkey; then they blasted a tunnel through the ridge so tractors could reach them. Drink it here rather than reading about it later.

Ston salt Worth it

Not a dish, but buy a bag. The saltworks below the walls are among the oldest continuously operating in Europe and still harvested by hand from shallow evaporation pans, exactly as they were in the fourteenth century. It is the commodity the walls were built to defend, and it costs almost nothing.

05Day 7 · Fri 14 AugDubrovnik

Dubrovnik

The most expensive eating on the route, with a genuine local cuisine hiding underneath the tourist menus.

Strategy

Dubrovnik has more bad expensive restaurants per square metre than anywhere else you will go. Two rules fix it. One: get off the Stradun. The alleys running uphill — Prijeko in particular — and the streets just outside Ploče Gate are where the food improves and the price drops. Two: order the Dubrovnik dishes, not the "Dalmatian" ones. Ragusa had its own cooking, distinct from the rest of the coast, and a kitchen that puts zelena menestra or šporki makaruli on the menu is telling you something about itself.

Dishes

Rožata Must try

Dubrovnik's own custard, EU protected, and much better than the crème caramel it resembles. The difference is rozulin — a rose-petal liqueur that has been made here for centuries and gives the set custard a faint, unmistakable floral edge. It has been recorded in Dubrovnik since at least the fifteenth century, which makes it older than most of the buildings around you.

Zelena menestra Must try

The genuine Dubrovnik home dish and the one that separates a real kitchen from a tourist one. Collards or kale slow-cooked with potato and several kinds of cured and smoked pork — sausage, pancetta, sometimes an ear — until everything collapses together. Rustic, salty, deeply savoury, and served at Christmas and family occasions. Heavy for August, but order it as a shared starter and you will understand the city better.

Šporki makaruli Worth it

"Dirty macaroni" — pasta in a long-cooked beef and tomato sauce enriched with cinnamon and clove, traditionally eaten at the feast of St Blaise, the city's patron. The spicing is the tell: it is a medieval Mediterranean sauce, not an Italian one, and the trade routes that brought those spices are the reason Ragusa existed.

Arancini Worth it

Not the Sicilian rice balls. In Dubrovnik arancini are strips of orange peel candied slowly in sugar syrup and rolled in sugar, sold in paper bags in the old town. A frugal Ragusan use of what was left after juicing, and a very good thing to have in the car.

06Days 7–10 · 14–17 AugKotor · Perast · Njeguši

The Bay of Kotor

Where the coast and the mountains meet on one plate — and where the single best cheap meal of the whole trip is a shack on the water.

Dishes

Njeguški pršut and Njeguški sir Must try

Eat this at source, on the Lovćen road, not in a Kotor restaurant. The village of Njeguši sits in a saddle at about 900 m where cold mountain air meets sea air rising off the bay — a specific microclimate that dries and smokes ham unlike anywhere else. It is smoked over beech, then air-dried, giving it a distinctly different character from Italian or Dalmatian prosciutto: firmer, smokier, saltier.

It comes with the local hard cow's cheese, often sir iz ulja — cheese matured submerged in oil — plus bread, olives, honey and a shot of loza. This is the plate that explains Montenegro.

How to do it — stop in Njeguši itself on the Lovćen drive. Roadside houses sell it; one of the producers below will show you the drying room.

Boka oysters, at the farm Must try

The bay farms its own oysters and mussels, and the best way to eat them is at a working farm rather than a restaurant — pulled out of the water, shucked in front of you, eaten at a plastic table on a pontoon. This is genuinely the highest pleasure-per-euro meal available on your entire route, and it is a fifteen-minute walk from Perast.

Kajmak Must try

Not cream, not butter, not cheese — the skin lifted off scalded milk, salted and left to mature in layers for days or weeks. Young kajmak is mild and spreadable; aged is sharp, salty, almost blue-cheesy. It goes on bread, on grilled meat, into ćevapi, onto cornmeal. You will meet it from here to Sarajevo and it is one of the genuinely distinctive foods of the region.

Njeguški steak Worth it

Veal or pork pounded flat, stuffed with njeguški pršut and the local cheese, rolled or folded, then pan-fried. It appears on every Montenegrin menu. Rich to the point of comedy; share it, or have it as your one big meal of the day.

Priganice Worth it

Small yeasted dough balls, deep fried, served hot with honey, kajmak and sometimes cheese. Eaten for breakfast, dessert or with drinks. The kajmak-and-honey combination — salty fresh dairy against raw honey on hot fried dough — is much better than the description suggests.

Perast cake Worth it

An almond cake specific to Perast, dense and marzipan-adjacent, made in a village of a few hundred people. Reviewers of the restaurant below name it unprompted as the reason to stay for dessert. It exists nowhere else.

Buzara and black risotto, again Skippable

Same dishes as Dalmatia, same quality, and you will have eaten both already. Order them here only if you loved them. Montenegro's distinctive food is uphill, not on the water.

Drink

Vranac Must try

Montenegro's native red — the name means "black stallion" — and it lives up to it: deep, tannic, dark-fruited, high in alcohol. The white counterpart is Krstač, a native grape named for the cross shape its bunches make. Most of both comes from Plantaže near Podgorica, whose main cellar, Šipčanik, is a converted underground aircraft hangar built to hide Yugoslav air force jets and now holding a couple of million litres in a tunnel designed to survive an air strike.

07Days 10–13 · 17–20 AugŽabljak · Durmitor

The mountains

Three nights at 1,456 m, where the food stops being Mediterranean entirely and becomes cornmeal, cream and lamb.

The shift

This is the most distinct cuisine on the route and the one people skip. Highland Montenegro has almost no olive oil, no seafood and no tomato tradition — it runs on dairy, maize, potato and sheep. Everything is heavier, and at this altitude, in a place that is genuinely cold at night in August, it makes complete sense. You have three nights here. Use them.

Dishes

Kačamak Must try

The definitive Durmitor dish. Cornmeal and potato boiled together, then beaten hard with kajmak and young cheese until the whole thing turns glossy and starts to pull away from the pot in strands. Served in the pan it was made in, with more kajmak melting on top. It is somewhere between polenta, mashed potato and fondue, and it has no real equivalent anywhere else in Europe.

Its close cousin cicvara is richer still — made with more kajmak and less potato, so the fat separates out into a golden pool. Both are traditionally a shepherd's meal. Order one for two people and something grilled alongside.

Lamb — boiled, or under the sač Must try

Two mountain preparations, both worth it. Jagnjetina ispod sača is the bell method again — lamb and potatoes under an iron dome buried in embers, three hours. Boiled lamb (jagnjetina u mlijeku, cooked in milk, or simply in its own broth) is the older highland version and sounds far less appealing than it is: the meat ends up falling apart and mild, with a rich broth served alongside. Reviewers of the restaurants below name the boiled lamb repeatedly and unprompted.

Order it a day ahead. Both methods take hours. Ask on your first night for your second.

Kajmak and skorup, properly Must try

You met kajmak on the coast; up here it is a different product. Skorup is the mature Durmitor version, aged in wooden vessels and sometimes for months — sharp, salty, funky, closer to a cheese than a cream. Sold from farms around Žabljak. If you like strong dairy, this is one of the most distinctive things you can eat in the Balkans.

Trout Worth it

From the Tara and the mountain lakes, usually grilled whole with a potato salad. The counterweight to everything else on this list, and a good idea on the third day when the dairy has caught up with you.

Suho meso and raštan Worth it

Suho meso is smoked, air-dried beef or mutton — the highland preserving tradition, sliced thin as a starter or dropped into stews for salt and smoke. Raštan is collard greens slow-cooked with it, the winter vegetable dish of the mountains.

Priganice with honey Worth it

The same fried dough as the coast, but here served with mountain honey and skorup, and usually for breakfast. Durmitor honey is worth buying — the flora at this altitude is entirely different from anything on the coast.

08Day 13 · Thu 20 AugMostar · Blagaj · Počitelj

Herzegovina

Four hundred years of Ottoman cooking, and two native grapes almost nobody outside the country has drunk.

Dishes

Ćevapi Must try

The national dish of the whole region, and Bosnia's version is the reference. Small fingers of minced beef (and often lamb), no filler, no egg, seasoned with little more than salt, garlic water and bicarbonate, rested, then grilled hard over charcoal. Ordered in fives or tens. What makes the Bosnian version distinct is the bread: a fresh somun, a soft flatbread, split open and pressed inside-down on the grill so the cut face soaks up the fat before the meat goes in.

Served with raw chopped onion and, if you ask, kajmak. Eaten with your hands. Do not order chips with it — nobody local does.

Klepe Must try

Bosnian ravioli: small pasta parcels of minced beef, boiled and then drowned in garlic yoghurt with melted butter and paprika poured over. A direct descendant of Turkish manti and one of the best things in the country. Almost never found outside the Balkans and rarely made properly even in Bosnia — if a menu has it, order it.

Begova čorba Must try

"Bey's soup" — chicken, okra, root vegetables and rice, thickened with a roux and finished with cream and egg yolk. The dish of the Ottoman gentry, still on every serious Bosnian menu. Thick, mild, faintly sour from the okra. It is the closest thing Bosnia has to a national soup and it is genuinely elegant.

Anything from an aščinica Must try

Not a dish but a category, and the most authentic meal available in Bosnia. An aščinica cooks braises and stews in the morning and sells them from trays until they run out — no grill, no menu, you point. What you will find: japrak (vine or chard leaves rolled around rice and meat), sogan-dolma (onions hollowed out and stuffed), bosanski lonac (a layered pot of meat and vegetables cooked for hours), sarma, stuffed peppers, and beans. Half the price of anywhere with a view of the bridge.

Bosnian coffee Must try

Locals will tell you why it is not Turkish coffee, and the difference is real: the water is boiled first, a little is set aside, the ground coffee is added to the pot and returned to the heat to foam, then the reserved water is poured back to settle it. It arrives as a set — džezva, fildžan, sugar cube, rahat lokum. Hold the sugar in your teeth and drink through it. Half an hour minimum.

Tufahija and the sweets Worth it

Tufahija is a whole apple poached in sugar syrup until translucent, cored and stuffed with walnuts, topped with cream — Ottoman, refined, and less aggressively sweet than it looks. Alongside it: hurmašice (syrup-soaked semolina biscuits), baklava, and kadaif (shredded pastry with walnut). Bosnian baklava is generally walnut rather than pistachio.

Uštipci and the breakfast plate Worth it

Uštipci are savoury fried dough balls served with kajmak and cheese — the Bosnian breakfast, and excellent before a long drive. The wider bosanski doručak plate adds cured meat, tomato, cheese and eggs.

Trout at Blagaj If time

The restaurants at the Buna source serve trout from the river, and the setting — under a two-hundred-metre cliff at one of Europe's largest karst springs — is the reason to sit down, not the food. Reviewers are consistent that the view is exceptional and the cooking is fine. Go for a coffee and the view; eat properly in Mostar.

Drink

Žilavka and Blatina Must try

The genuine discovery of this leg. Herzegovina grows two indigenous grapes on hot karst around Mostar and Čitluk, and almost none of it is exported. Žilavka is the white — mineral, high-acid, faintly bitter-almond on the finish, and much more serious than its obscurity suggests. Blatina is the red, and it has a genuine botanical oddity: its flowers are functionally female and cannot self-pollinate, so every Blatina vineyard must be interplanted with a pollinator variety. A monoculture Blatina vineyard is literally impossible.

For a foodie — this is the wine on the whole route you are least likely ever to encounter again. Buy a bottle of each to bring home.

Rakija, Bosnian-style Worth it

Inland, the base changes from grape to plum — šljivovica. Herzegovina, being Mediterranean, still makes loza from grapes, so on this leg you can drink both and taste the geography change.

09Day 14 · Fri 21 AugSarajevo

Sarajevo

One night, and the best street food on the route. Plan it properly and you can eat four times.

One night only

You have a single evening and a morning here. The efficient plan: ćevapi from a ćevabdžinica for dinner, Bosnian coffee in a courtyard afterwards, burek by weight for breakfast, and a sit-down lunch only if the schedule allows. Do not spend the one evening in a restaurant with a menu in five languages.

Dishes

Sarajevo ćevapi Must try

The city's ćevapi are their own sub-tradition and Bosnians argue about the ranking with real heat. Sarajevo style: smaller, joined in pairs or fours on the grill and separated on the plate, served in somun with raw onion, with kajmak on request. Ordered by number — five or ten. The great ćevabdžinice are on Bravadžiluk, the street of coppersmiths running east from Baščaršija, and several have been there for generations.

Burek, by weight, from a proper buregdžinica Must try

Hand-rolled filo, coiled into a spiral in a round pan, baked — traditionally under a sač, a metal dome covered with hot coals, which is where one of the best places in the city gets its name. You order by weight: "trista grama" is 300 g and a generous portion. It comes with kiselo mlijeko (sour milk) or yoghurt poured over or alongside, which is not optional if you want it done right.

Remember the naming: burek is meat, sirnica cheese, zeljanica spinach and cheese, krompiruša potato. The meat one frequently sells out by early afternoon.

Coffee in a Baščaršija courtyard Must try

Not a dish, an hour. The right version is a courtyard off the main tourist lane, full of locals, where a Bosnian coffee costs about 3.50 KM and nobody will hurry you. Be warned that a genuinely local coffee house also means a genuinely smoky one.

Dolma, japrak and the aščinica dishes Worth it

Same category as Mostar, done at a higher level here — Sarajevo is where Ottoman court cooking actually lived. If you have a lunch slot, this is the better use of it than another grill.

Tufahija and Sarajevsko pivo Worth it

The city's dessert and the city's beer. Sarajevsko has been brewed since 1864, and the brewery matters beyond the beer: it sits over a natural spring, and during the siege — when the water supply was cut — it became one of the city's main water points. People queued there under fire, and some were killed doing it.

A thick, pale, mildly fermented millet drink, faintly sour and faintly sweet, sold in the old town and drunk as a pick-me-up. It tastes like nothing else you have had and reactions divide sharply. Costs almost nothing; worth one glass for the experience.

10Days 15–16 · 22–23 AugSalzburg

Salzburg

The last dinner, a dessert shaped like three mountains, and a piece of street food improbably named after the country you just left.

Dishes

Salzburger Nockerl Must try

The city's own dessert and a proper piece of theatre. A sweet soufflé of whipped egg white, sugar and a little flour, piled into three tall peaks — representing the three hills around Salzburg, the Mönchsberg, Kapuzinerberg and Gaisberg — dusted heavily with icing sugar to look like snow, and baked until barely set. It arrives enormous, collapses within minutes, and tastes of almost nothing but sweet air.

Practical — takes 20–25 minutes to bake, so order it with your main. Almost always sized for two or three. Order it once, share it, and photograph it fast.

A Bosna, in Salzburg Must try

The best joke on your entire itinerary. Salzburg's own street food, invented here in the late 1940s, is called a Bosna: a grilled bratwurst in a crisp white roll with raw onion, mustard, parsley and — the defining element — a heavy dusting of curry powder. It is sold from small windows on and around the Getreidegasse and it is what Salzburgers eat after a night out.

You will have spent four days actually in Bosnia, eating ćevapi that look nothing like this, and then arrive in Austria to find a curry-dusted sausage bearing the name. Nobody agrees on where the name came from. Eat one.

Mozartkugel — but the original Must try

Worth doing properly because the difference is real. The original Mozartkugel was created in 1890 by Paul Fürst, a Salzburg confectioner: pistachio marzipan centre, nougat around it, hand-dipped in dark chocolate on a stick, then the stick removed and the hole filled. His descendants still make them by hand in the same shop, wrapped in silver and blue.

Everything in red and gold — the ones in every airport — is a mass-produced industrial version by a different company, with a different recipe and milk chocolate. The Fürst originals are sold loose, cost several times more, and are a completely different confection. Buy a small bag of the real ones.

Wiener Schnitzel, and the distinction that matters Worth it

Legally in Austria, Wiener Schnitzel means veal. If it is pork it must be called Schnitzel Wiener Art — "in the Viennese style" — and it costs about half as much. Both are good; menus are explicit about which is which, and now you can read them. Properly made, the crumb should puff and separate slightly from the meat, which is achieved by shaking the pan so hot fat washes over the top.

Kasnocken Worth it

The Alpine one: small soft dumplings tossed with mountain cheese and buried under fried onions. The Austrian sibling of Käsespätzle, and the correct thing to eat if the evening turns cold. Heavy, salty, superb with beer.

Kaiserschmarrn Worth it

The shredded, caramelised pancake with plum compote, named for Franz Joseph. If you skipped it in Munich, this is the second and last chance — and it is arguably more at home here.

Salzburg salt If time

The commodity the city is named for, still mined at the Dürrnberg above Hallein twenty minutes south. Buy a box as the closing bookend to the Ston salt pans you walked past on Day 6 — the two ends of the same medieval trade.

Drink

Beer from a wooden barrel, in a monastery Must try

Augustiner Bräu Mülln has been brewing since 1621 and the serving ritual is unchanged and unlike anywhere else. You buy a token, take a heavy stone mug from the shelf, rinse it yourself at the stone fountain, hand it to the man at the barrel, and he fills it straight from the wood. Food is not ordered from a menu — a market arcade of stalls sells pork belly, pretzels, radishes and fish, and you carry it to a bench in a hall or the chestnut garden.

It is the same institution as the Munich beer garden at the other end of your trip, several centuries older, and it makes a very neat closing bracket. Salzburg's other brewery is Stiegl, founded 1492 and the largest private brewery in Austria.
