00Before you cross

Six things that make the rest of the trip make sense

Money, borders, language and driving change under you four times. Here's the shape of it.

Money

On the ground

Germany, Austria, Slovenia and Croatia are all euro (Croatia joined 1 January 2023, so no more kuna). Montenegro is the strange one — it uses the euro but is not in the EU and never joined the eurozone. It adopted the Deutsche Mark unilaterally in 1999 to escape the collapsing Yugoslav dinar, then switched to euros in 2002 when the Mark disappeared, without asking anyone's permission. The ECB has never been thrilled about it.

Bosnia is the one to plan for. The currency is the konvertibilna marka (KM, code BAM), pegged to the euro at a fixed 1.95583 — which is exactly the old Deutsche Mark rate, because the peg was inherited from the Mark in 1998. Euros are widely accepted in tourist spots at a rounded, unfavourable rate. Draw KM from an ATM in Mostar or Sarajevo and use it, especially for coffee, ćevapi and anything at a roadside stop.

Borders

On the ground

Slovenia and Croatia are both in Schengen now, so the Austria→Slovenia→Croatia run should be uninterrupted. The real borders are the two exits from the EU: Croatia→Montenegro and Montenegro→Bosnia, then Bosnia→Croatia on the way north. Passports get checked and stamped.

Debeli Brijeg (Croatia→Montenegro, the coastal crossing above Herceg Novi) is the one with a reputation for August queues — it is the single funnel for everyone driving the coast. Early morning is the standard fix.

Heads up

Šćepan Polje (Montenegro→Bosnia, up the Tara/Piva) is a small mountain crossing on a narrow road. Small crossings can have restricted opening hours and can be closed for works. verify before committing — if it is shut you are looking at a long backtrack through Nikšić.

Language

Local knowledge

Croatian, Bosnian, Montenegrin and Serbian are, linguistically, one language with four political names and mutually intelligible everywhere on this route. What changes is the alphabet. Croatia and the Bosniak-Croat Federation use Latin script. Republika Srpska — which includes Foča, Tjentište and the whole Sutjeska corridor — uses Cyrillic, and you will hit road signs where the Latin has been painted out. Being able to read Cyrillic place names for a few hours is genuinely useful: Фоча = Foča, Тјентиште = Tjentište, Никшић = Nikšić, Сарајево = Sarajevo.

Six words carry you a long way: dobar dan good day · hvala thanks · molim please/you're welcome · izvinite excuse me · račun the bill · živjeli cheers. In Bosnia you will also hear the Ottoman-derived merhaba as a greeting and selam as an informal hello.

Coffee, and how to order food without being obvious

Local knowledge

Burek is meat. Only meat. This is the single most useful thing to know in Bosnia. The filled pastries are named by filling: burek (meat), sirnica (cheese), zeljanica (spinach and cheese), krompiruša (potato). Asking for a "cheese burek" is the tell. In Croatia the word has drifted and burek can mean any of them — so it changes at the border.

Bosnian coffee is not Turkish coffee and locals will tell you why. It is served in a džezva (small copper pot) with a fildžan (handleless cup), a sugar cube and usually a piece of rahat lokum. The ritual: pour a little, let the grounds settle, hold the sugar cube in your teeth and drink through it, or dip it. It takes half an hour. That is the point of it — coffee here is a unit of time, not a drink. Do not order one if you are leaving in ten minutes.

Rakija is the regional fruit brandy — plum (šljivovica) in Bosnia and Serbia, grape (loza) on the coast, herb-infused (travarica) in Dalmatia. Eye contact when you clink, and it is a sipping drink despite everything about it suggesting otherwise.

Driving

On the ground
  • Headlights on during the day are required year-round in Slovenia, Montenegro and Bosnia. Croatia requires them in the winter half of the year, but running them permanently costs you nothing.
  • Alcohol limits are lower than the Netherlands. Montenegro and Bosnia sit around 0.03%, Slovenia and Croatia 0.05%. Treat it as zero.
  • Austria and Slovenia need vignettes. Austria additionally has separate section tolls (Sondermaut) on specific alpine tunnels — Karawanken, Tauern, Katschberg — paid at a barrier as you arrive. Croatia is a ticket-and-pay motorway system. Montenegro's only toll is the Sozina tunnel, which is not on your route. Bosnia has tolls on the newer A1 motorway sections.
  • Green Card is the document that matters for Montenegro and Bosnia — both sit outside the EU insurance system. Yours covers both.

The one open decision that reshapes the back half

Heads up

Day 13, Žabljak → Mostar, is still an open A/B/C choice, and it is not only a road choice — it decides whether you see Sutjeska at all, and it can change the whole route home.

  • A — Nikšić → Trebinje. No Sutjeska. The least interesting road of the three, and the low-risk default.
  • B — Podgorica → Trebinje. No Sutjeska either, but the safest on charge: near-continuous descent off the plateau, and Podgorica is the strongest hub in the country.
  • C — Šćepan Polje → Sutjeska → Foča. The best road on the trip and the only one that reaches Tjentište — but it does not reach Mostar in a day. It flips Day 13 into a Sutjeska overnight, Day 14 into Foča → Sarajevo → Konjic → Mostar, and pushes the exit south past Počitelj onto the Croatian A1.

So: read section 15 as optional, and sections 17 and 18 as order-swappable. Everything else here holds whichever way you go.

The EV problem, stated plainly

Heads up

Charging density collapses roughly at the Montenegrin border and does not recover until Sarajevo. The one genuinely unanswered question is what charges in Žabljak — but you have three nights booked there, so an overnight household socket at ~2.3 kW over ten hours (call it 120–150 km a night, three nights running) may quietly solve it without any DC at all. Confirm the socket on arrival, not on departure. The Kotor → Nikšić → Šćepan Polje → Foča → Tjentište → Mostar corridor is the thin part of the whole trip: mountain roads, heavy elevation change, air conditioning running, and long gaps between anything faster than a household socket.

The practical mitigations are the ones you already have: arrive at each of Žabljak, Tjentište and Mostar with real margin rather than a calculated one; treat overnight AC charging at accommodation as the primary plan and DC as a bonus; carry the CEE red 16A/32A, CEE blue and Schuko adapters plus your own Type 2 cable, since a surprising number of AC points in the region are untethered; and trust PlugShare check-in dates over Tesla's map data, which is sparse south of Croatia. Mountain driving also gives back more regen on descents than a flat-country range estimate expects — the Lovćen and Durmitor descents in particular.

01Day 1 · Sat 8 AugRotterdam → Maria Laach → Munich · ~8 h

Maria Laach, then Munich

A city that exists because a duke burned down a bridge to steal a toll.

History

Maria Laach stands on a volcano. The Laacher See is a caldera, left by an eruption about 13,000 years ago that was among the largest in Europe in the last hundred thousand years — it threw ash as far as Sweden and northern Italy and dammed the Rhine. The volcanic field beneath it is classed as dormant rather than extinct, and carbon dioxide still bubbles up through the water along the southeastern shore, where you can watch it break the surface from the path.

The abbey was begun in 1093 by Count Palatine Henry II as a burial church, and it is one of the most complete Romanesque buildings in Germany — six towers, almost untouched by later fashion. The reason is money: the community was poor at exactly the moments everyone else was rebuilding in Gothic and then Baroque, so nothing got improved. The arcaded forecourt at the west end, the paradise, is an Italian idea and rare this far north. If you have twenty minutes, that is the thing to stand in.

History

In 1158 Henry the Lion, Duke of Saxony and Bavaria, had a problem: the Bishop of Freising owned the bridge over the Isar at Oberföhring and collected the toll on the salt road. Henry burned it down, built his own bridge a few kilometres upstream, and forced the traffic through his settlement. The Emperor Frederick Barbarossa arbitrated, and — in one of history's more brazen rulings — let Henry keep the toll on condition he paid the bishop a third. That settlement is Munich. The name comes from Munichen, "by the monks," after the Benedictines already living there; the monk still sits on the city's coat of arms.

The Wittelsbachs took Bavaria in 1180 and did not let go until 1918 — 738 years, one of the longest unbroken dynastic runs in European history. Almost everything monumental in the city centre is theirs.

Local knowledge

You are legally allowed to bring your own food into a Munich beer garden. This is not a loophole, it is settled law with a real origin. Brewers dug cellars along the Isar to keep beer cool and planted chestnut trees over them for shade; when they started serving on top of the cellars, the city's innkeepers objected to the competition. The compromise, formalised under Ludwig I, was that beer gardens could sell beer but not food — so customers brought their own. The food ban has gone, but the right to bring your own remains, and locals still turn up with bread, radishes and a tablecloth. It only applies in the self-service (Selbstbedienung) section, not the table-service one.

On the ground

The Eisbach standing wave in the Englischer Garten, just past the Haus der Kunst, has surfers on it in all weather and at most hours. It is a genuine river wave, not a machine, formed by concrete blocks under the surface, and it has been ridden since the 1970s. Free, permanently entertaining, and about ten minutes' walk from the Altstadt.

The Glockenspiel on the Neues Rathaus is the classic disappointment of Munich — a fifteen-minute clockwork show that most visitors find slower than advertised. The Viktualienmarkt, two minutes away, is the better use of the same time.

Reinheitsgebot, the beer purity law limiting beer to water, barley and hops, was issued in Bavaria in 1516 — partly for quality, partly to stop brewers competing with bakers for wheat and rye. Yeast was added to the list only after Pasteur.

Heads up

Two Superchargers are planned into this leg — Koblenz, roughly 25 minutes past the abbey, then Frankfurt Flughafen straight on the A3. That plus the abbey is the whole day's budget. Do not add a third thing.

Munich's city centre is an Umweltzone (low-emission zone). Fully electric vehicles are exempt and need no sticker. German cities are separately rolling out diesel restrictions that do not concern you at all.

02Day 2 · Sun 9 AugMunich → Bled · the Alpine crossing

Over the Karawanks

Three ways through the same wall of rock, and one of them is a war grave.

On the ground

You come down through Bavaria into Austria, and the Alps run east–west across your path. The Karawanken range is the last barrier before Slovenia and there are three ways over or under it:

  • Karawanken Tunnel (A11/A2) — 7.9 km, opened 1991, motorway the whole way. A section toll paid at the barrier, no advance purchase. Fastest, dullest, and the correct choice if you are tired.
  • Wurzen Pass — short but brutally steep, with sustained gradients around 18%. Spectacular, and it drops you at Kranjska Gora at the foot of the Vršič road.
  • Loibl Pass — a tunnel under the ridge with hairpins either side.

For an EV, the steep passes are less punishing than they look on paper: you spend on the climb and get a real fraction of it back on the descent.

History

The Loibl Pass road tunnel was cut through the mountain between 1943 and 1945 by prisoners of Loibl, a satellite camp of Mauthausen, working from both the Austrian and Slovenian sides. Several hundred died. There are memorials at both portals. It is the first of many places on this trip where an ordinary piece of infrastructure turns out to have been built by people who did not survive it — a pattern you will see again at Vršič and at Đurđevića Tara.

Heads up

Slovenia requires a vignette from the moment you touch a motorway or expressway. Austria's digital vignette may have an activation delay rule — the fraud-prevention change means a digital vignette bought minutes before you drive may not be valid immediately. A physical sticker is valid on application. verify current rule at asfinag.at

History

The Karawanks are a real boundary, not just a range. The ridge you tunnel under is the geological seam between the Eastern Alps and the Dinarides — and it has been a linguistic and political frontier for well over a thousand years. North of it, German. South of it, Slovene. The line has moved remarkably little since the early medieval period, which is unusual in Europe.

It was settled by a vote. After the First World War, with the Habsburg empire dissolved, both Austria and the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes claimed the Carinthian basin. Rather than let it be decided by force, a plebiscite was held in October 1920. The southern, Slovene-majority zone voted first — and, to nearly everyone's surprise, voted to stay with Austria by roughly 59 to 41, meaning a substantial number of Slovene speakers chose Vienna over Belgrade. The second zone never voted; the result stood. It remains one of the very few borders in central Europe drawn by ballot rather than by army, and there is still a Slovene-speaking minority in Carinthia on the Austrian side.

Local knowledge

Slovenia in one paragraph. Two million people, an area smaller than Wales, and it contains Alps, karst, Mediterranean coast and Pannonian plain — which is a genuinely absurd amount of geography for the size. Over half the country is forest, one of the highest proportions in Europe, and there are brown bears in it, several hundred of them. It was the first Yugoslav republic to leave, in 1991, in a war that lasted ten days and cost fewer than seventy lives — which is why Slovenia feels so unlike everywhere else you are going on this trip. It joined the EU in 2004 and the euro in 2007.

Practical: the vignette is electronic, checked by camera, and buying it at a petrol station before the border is the simplest path. Slovene motorways are excellent and short. Coverage for charging is dense enough that this is the last easy country until Austria on the way home.

03Days 2–3 · 9–11 AugBled · camped at Kamna Gorica

Lake Bled

The most photographed lake in the Alps, and a set of rowing rights inherited from an empress.

On the ground

You are camped at Kamna Gorica, about ten minutes out toward Radovljica, for two nights — which is the better call than a lakeside pitch in mid-August. Bled is a day-visit; the camp is the base. There is more on Kamna Gorica and Radovljica in the next section.

History

Bled Castle is first documented in 1011, when Emperor Henry II handed the estate to the Bishops of Brixen — a South Tyrolean bishopric that then owned this valley, at arm's length, for over eight hundred years. It is the oldest castle in Slovenia and the reason the lake was never simply farmland.

The island church is dedicated to the Assumption of Mary, but the site was a pre-Christian shrine before that — excavations under the church found early Slavic graves and evidence of a temple. The overlay is literal: a Christian church built directly on top of a pagan sanctuary, on an island, in a lake.

Local knowledge

The pletna boats are a hereditary monopoly. The flat-bottomed wooden boats that ferry people to the island are rowed standing, with two oars, by pletnarji whose right to do it was granted by Empress Maria Theresa in 1740 to twenty-two families from the village of Mlino. The right still passes down through those families. If the man rowing you looks like he was born to it, that is because his great-great-great-grandfather was licensed for it by the Habsburgs.

The wishing bell. The island church's bell comes with a story: a widow, grieving her murdered husband, had a bell cast for the church; the boat carrying it sank in a storm and the bell went to the bottom of the lake, where it is said still to ring in bad weather. She went to Rome and became a nun, and after her death the Pope sent a replacement bell — the one you ring today. Ring it and make a wish, which is what everyone in the queue behind you is also doing.

Kremšnita, the Bled cream cake, is a specific standardised recipe — custard and cream between two layers of pastry, cut in a square — codified in 1953 by Ištvan Lukačević, then pastry chef at the Hotel Park. The Park still claims the original. It is one of the very few desserts in Europe with a documented inventor and a fixed spec.

On the ground

The photograph everybody wants — island, church, mountains behind — is taken from Mala Osojnica, on the far side of the lake from the town. It is short but genuinely steep, with metal stair sections near the top, roughly 20–30 minutes of hard uphill from the lakeside path. Ojstrica is the lower, easier viewpoint on the same hillside and gives about 80% of the same shot for half the effort. Both are best at sunrise, when the lake is glass and the tour buses have not arrived.

Swimming is allowed and the water is warm — Bled sits over thermal springs, which is why the lake reaches genuinely pleasant temperatures by August despite the altitude. There are no motorboats permitted anywhere on it, which is why it is so quiet.

Vintgar Gorge, 4 km north, is a wooden walkway pinned to the rock walls of the Radovna river gorge, built in 1893. It is beautiful and it is also a single-file boardwalk that gets fully congested in August. Timed entry tickets are the norm now. check booking requirement

History

Vila Bled, on the south shore, was Tito's summer residence. He entertained Nehru, Nasser, Ho Chi Minh and Haile Selassie here. The Non-Aligned Movement — the third bloc that let Yugoslavia sit outside both NATO and the Warsaw Pact — was substantially built in conversations at Bled and Brijuni. That independent position is the reason Yugoslavs could travel freely west in the 1960s while other Eastern Europeans could not, and it is background you will need when you get to Sarajevo.

History

The pletna and the rowing rights. The flat-bottomed boats that ferry people to the island are not a tourist invention. In 1740 Maria Theresa granted the families of Mlino the exclusive right to row pilgrims to the island church — a privilege that has been inherited down family lines ever since. There are a limited number of licensed pletnarji, the right passes from father to son, and outsiders cannot buy in. The boatmen row standing, with two oars, in a stroke specific to this lake.

On the island: the church of the Assumption sits on what was a pre-Christian shrine — excavations found a substantial early Slavic burial ground beneath it. The tradition is that a groom carries his bride up the 99 steps and that she stays silent throughout. Inside, you ring the wishing bell; the legend attached to it involves a widow, a drowned dowry, and a bell that was lost in the lake, and it is told slightly differently by everyone.

On the ground

The viewpoints, ranked. Both are reached from the Velika Zaka end of the lake, and both are steep, rooty and slippery after rain.

  • Ojstrica (611 m) — 20–25 minutes up. The lower of the two and, counterintuitively, the better composition: island centred, castle behind. This is the shot everybody knows.
  • Mala Osojnica (685 m) — another 15–20 minutes via a steep metal staircase with a handrail. Wider view taking in more of the Julian Alps, less iconic framing.

Both face roughly east, so sunrise gives mist and backlight, late afternoon gives clean front-lit stone on the church and castle. From Bled town on foot it is about 3 km round the north shore to the trailhead — shorter and quieter than the Mlino side, which is better saved for the walk back.

You cannot take a boat one way. Pletnas run round trips to the island only; rowboats must return to where you hired them; motorboats are banned outright on the lake. Velika Zaka has its own pletna stand and rowboat hire, plus the best swimming beach on the lake — shallow pebble entry, changing facilities, a bar. Water runs 22–24°C in August.

Local knowledge

Kremna rezina — the Bled cream cake — is a specific, standardised object: a square of custard and whipped cream between two layers of pastry, developed in its current form at the Hotel Park in 1953 and made to a fixed recipe with a fixed height. Millions have been sold. Eat one at the source rather than a supermarket copy.

Vintgar Gorge, a few kilometres away, is a 1.6 km boardwalk pinned to the cliff above the Radovna, ending at the Šum waterfall — about 45 minutes each way, or a 60–90 minute forest loop back via Hom for the quieter return. It is timed-entry, booked online, capped daily, and sells out in August, and there is a park-and-ride shuttle from Podhom rather than driving to the entrance. Gates close in the late afternoon, which makes it a morning stop, not an afternoon one. book the slot before you leave; confirm closing time and shuttle timetable

The quieter alternative is Pokljuka Gorge — dry, limestone, natural rock bridges and a cave, and a fraction of the traffic because there is no turquoise river in it.

04Day 3 · Mon 10 AugVršič Pass and the upper Soča, from Kamna Gorica

The Russian Road

Fifty hairpins over the Julian Alps on a road that exists for one of the worst reasons a road can exist — down into a valley that was, for two and a half years, one of the deadliest places on earth, and which now holds the most beautiful river in Europe.

History

Nobody would build this road for pleasure. The fifty bends you will drive today were cut in 1915 by the Austro-Hungarian army for one purpose: to move men, mules, guns and shells from the railhead at Kranjska Gora over the mountains and down into the Soča valley, where the empire was fighting Italy for its life.

Before the war, crossing here meant a mule track. When Italy declared war in May 1915, the Soča became a front line overnight, and the only supply routes to it ran the long way round. A road over Vršič would cut days off the journey. It had to be built immediately, in mountains, at altitude, in wartime — which meant it had to be built by people who had no choice.

That labour was Russian. Prisoners taken on the Eastern Front were shipped west and put to work on the pass in their thousands, alongside other POWs and conscripted civilians. They worked through the winter of 1915–16 in inadequate clothing, at altitude, on rations that were poor even by the standards of a starving empire. The Slovene name for the road — Ruska cesta, the Russian Road — is not decoration. It is an accurate description of who built it.

On 8 March 1916 an avalanche came off the slopes above the northern workings and buried a camp and the men in it. The figure usually cited is around a hundred; accounts vary widely and the true number was probably never established. It was not the only avalanche and not the only camp. Deaths from cold, exhaustion and disease across the two construction seasons ran far higher than any single incident.

The survivors built a chapel that same year in the Orthodox style they knew — timber, twin onion domes, small enough to feel domestic. It stands at hairpin 8 on the northern climb, still tended, with an annual Slovene–Russian commemoration.

On the ground

Stop at the chapel on the way up, not the way down. It is a small pull-off with a wooden building set back in the trees and it is easy to drive straight past. Ten minutes. The mood of the whole road changes once you know what you are driving on.

The bends are numbered with stone markers giving the altitude — 24 up from Kranjska Gora, 26 down into Trenta. The northern climb is cobbled with the original hand-laid setts. When the tyres go loose and loud under you on the corners, that is their work you are driving on.

History

What it was built for. Italy entered the war having been promised Trentino, Trieste, Istria and a slice of Dalmatia under the secret Treaty of London. To collect, it had to break Austro-Hungarian lines along the Soča — the Isonzo — a front of roughly ninety kilometres running from Mount Rombon above Bovec down to the Adriatic.

The Italians attacked eleven times. The battles are numbered rather than named, First through Eleventh Isonzo, which tells you something in itself: they were not distinct manoeuvres so much as repeated attempts at the same thing. Against them, Austro-Hungarian troops held high ground in terrain that favoured defence absolutely. Positions were hacked into rock at two thousand metres. Men fought and froze in tunnels cut through glacier ice. Shells bursting on limestone threw stone splinters as lethal as the shrapnel itself.

The Twelfth was different. In October 1917, reinforced by German divisions and using new infiltration tactics along with poison gas, the Central Powers broke the line near Kobarid — Caporetto. The Italian Second Army collapsed and the retreat did not stop until the Piave, more than a hundred kilometres back. Caporetto entered Italian as a word for total, humiliating defeat and is still used that way.

A young German lieutenant named Erwin Rommel made his reputation there, taking Mount Matajur with a small detachment and winning the Pour le Mérite for it. Hemingway drove ambulances on this front and set A Farewell to Arms around the Caporetto retreat — the river his characters cross is the one you will be swimming in.

Estimates of the dead across the whole Isonzo Front run to several hundred thousand on each side, Italian losses the heavier. The bones are still coming out of the mountains; every few years a glacier or a landslide gives up equipment, ordnance or men.

Local knowledge

Two things to spot from the car on the way up. Prisojnik is the mountain on your left as you gain height, and it has two features people drive straight past.

High in its north face there is a natural rock arch — Prisankovo okno, the Prisojnik Window — a hole punched clean through the ridge, tens of metres across. From the right hairpin you can see daylight through the mountain.

Lower on the same face the rock resolves into a human profile: a woman looking out over the valley. She is the Ajdovska deklica, the Pagan Girl, a mountain spirit who foretold a newborn's fate and was turned to stone by her sisters for revealing the future to mortals. Like all such faces, until you see her you will swear there is nothing there. There is usually a signboard at the relevant bend.

The sharp curved horn visible from Slemenova špica is Jalovec (2,645 m) — the emblem of the Slovenian Alpine Association, the profile on the badge of every mountaineering club in the country. Slovenes recognise it instantly and are usually pleased when foreigners do.

On the ground

Slemenova špica (1,911 m) is the walk, and the payoff is out of all proportion to the effort — which is why it is one of the most photographed spots in Slovenia and why you want to be on it early.

The trail leaves the Vršič car park, climbs through larch and dwarf pine, and then does the thing that makes it special: it stops climbing and opens out. You come over a lip onto a broad tilted meadow and the mountains are simply there — Jalovec dead ahead, Mangart off west, the whole Trenta basin dropping away below. No summit scramble, no exposure, no scree. Roughly 1h30 up, 1h15 down.

Two small tarns sit in the grass and hold reflections when the wind drops. Take a warm layer even if it is twenty-eight degrees at camp — nineteen hundred metres with wind on it is a different climate.

Heads up

Storms build over these mountains from about two in the afternoon in summer, and they arrive faster than you expect. Slemenova špica is an open high meadow with nothing above it. That is the whole reason the walk goes in the morning. If cloud is stacking on the ridges while you climb, turn round.

Parking at the top is limited — a small area serving the two huts — and it fills early in August. Triglav National Park has been tightening vehicle access across several sites in recent years. confirm current Vršič parking rules and any summer restriction with the campsite or tnp.si before setting the alarm

History

Trenta was a hard place to live. Resist the idea that it was always beautiful to be here. For most of its history this was an isolated, marginal valley — too high and narrow for real farming, cut off by snow for months, reachable only on foot.

Iron ore was mined and smelted from the medieval period, charcoal burned in the forests to feed the furnaces. When the ore ran out the valley fell back on livestock, hunting, and a peculiar local trade: herbs. Trenta men went high after medicinal plants and walked them out over the passes into Italy and Austria to sell. Some developed enough knowledge to be treated as healers — the trentarski zdravniki, the Trenta doctors. Not physicians in any formal sense, but men whose alpine botany was real and hard-won.

The valley's famous outsider was Julius Kugy (1858–1944), a Trieste merchant, mountaineer, botanist and writer. Kugy came to Trenta chasing a flower — Scabiosa trenta, described once in an eighteenth-century account and never reliably found since. He never found it either. What he found instead was the Julian Alps, and he spent a long life climbing them and writing about them with a romanticism that shaped how Slovenes see these mountains. Crucially he wrote about local guides as people rather than as equipment, which was not the norm for gentleman-alpinists of his era. His bronze monument sits below the pass on the Trenta side, looking up at the peaks.

Kugy's guide was a Trenta hunter called Anton Tožbar, nicknamed Špik. In 1873 Tožbar was attacked by a bear and had his lower jaw torn away. He survived, was fitted with a crude prosthesis, and went back to hunting and guiding. He is buried in Trenta.

Local knowledge

Zlatorog — the story to tell in the car. You will see the name everywhere, on beer bottles and signs and buildings, and it comes from here.

Zlatorog was a white chamois with horns of gold who guarded a paradise garden high in the Triglav massif and a treasure beneath it. The horns were the key. A young hunter from Trenta — poor, in love with a girl who wanted gold — went up after him and shot him. Where the blood fell a Triglav rose sprang up; the wounded animal ate the flower and was instantly healed, and when he rose the sun caught his golden horns and dazzled the hunter, who fell from the cliff to his death. Zlatorog, betrayed, destroyed the garden in his grief and left. What had been paradise became the bare rock and scree of the high Julians, and has stayed that way.

It is a story about greed ruining a place, told by people living in a landscape that looks exactly as though something once ruined it. Collected and published in the nineteenth century, it became foundational to Slovene national romanticism. Whether Laško brewery's use of the name honours that or flattens it is a matter of taste, but you will be drinking it either way.

History

The source, and why the water is that colour. Most great rivers begin ambiguously — a bog, a confluence, a spread of snowmelt where nobody can point at one spot. The Soča does not. It comes out of a hole in a cliff.

Behind the rock face is a karst cave holding a subterranean lake, fed by water travelling underground from the Zadnjica basin and the surrounding limestone. From here the river runs 138 km to the Adriatic, crossing into Italy and finishing near Monfalcone under its Italian name, the Isonzo.

The colour is not an artefact of photography. The mechanism is suspended limestone — water moving through karst carries extremely fine calcium carbonate particles, rock ground to flour, small enough to stay in suspension and to scatter light at the blue-green end of the spectrum. Add exceptional clarity, a pale limestone bed reflecting light back up, and depth in the pools. Unusually, the Soča holds the colour along nearly its whole length — most rivers lose it within a few kilometres of source.

It is also 9–12°C all summer and does not warm up meaningfully in August. Swimming in it is a short, sharp, entirely worthwhile experience.

Heads up

The last hundred metres to the spring itself is not a walk. The path from the car park is easy; the final approach is a short, steep, genuinely exposed scramble on fixed steel cables, above a drop, on rock that is wet because there is a river coming out of it. People do it in trainers and people get hurt. There is a viewpoint short of the cables that shows the cave mouth perfectly well — stopping there is a reasonable decision, not a failure of nerve.

Parking is a handful of roadside spaces. In August you may have to wait, or park lower and walk up.

Local knowledge

The marble trout. The river holds Salmo marmoratus, a large mottled salmonid endemic to this Adriatic drainage and one of the rarest in Europe. It was pushed to the edge not by pollution or overfishing but by hybridisation — brown trout stocked through the twentieth century interbred with it until pure populations survived in only a handful of isolated headwater streams. A long-running conservation programme has been restoring them. Fishing here is tightly permitted; the upper reaches are catch-and-release, barbless hooks.

On the ground

Velika korita — the Great Gorge — is the swim. Below the Lepena confluence the river does something strange: instead of spreading across its bed it disappears into a slot it has cut for itself in the limestone, several hundred metres long, only a few metres wide, dropping to something like fifteen metres deep in places.

The rock has been polished by water and stone into shapes that look sculpted — smooth flutes, scalloped hollows, potholes drilled out by pebbles spinning in eddies over thousands of years. Footbridges cross it, and the view straight down into the slot is the reason to come. There are pools at either end where you can get in.

Heads up

That polish means the slabs are slick, especially where damp, and there are unprotected edges above a deep narrow channel with fast water in it. People fall here. Move deliberately, keep back from the lip when framing a shot, and do not jump into water you have not looked into first — depth and submerged rock vary enormously along the gorge.

Local knowledge

Eat the valley's own food, not pizza. The upper Soča has a cuisine distinct from both the Slovene interior and the Italian side: cheese, potato, buckwheat, dairy, walnuts.

  • Bovški sir — hard sheep's-milk cheese from the pastures above Bovec, protected designation. Sharp, slightly piquant. The base of most of what follows.
  • Frika — the local dish. Bovški sir fried into a flat cake, alone or bound with grated potato, until the outside crisps and the inside goes molten.
  • Čompe an skuta — boiled potatoes in their skins with curd cheese, oil and pepper. Peasant food in the honest sense.
  • Kobariški štruklji — walnut-and-raisin dumplings rolled in fried breadcrumbs, from Kobarid down the valley. Protected designation.
  • Trout — grilled whole, farmed rather than wild given the conservation status.

Mountain huts (koče) serve soup, sausage, štruklji, strudel — and are cash only. The two at the top of the pass are among the most accessible in the country.

Local knowledge

Where you are actually staying. Kamna Gorica is not a random village. It was an iron village — from the Middle Ages until the nineteenth century it forged nails, and water channels were cut through the settlement to drive the hammers. You can still see the channels running between the houses. It is a protected heritage settlement and almost nobody staying at Bled has heard of it.

Ten minutes away, Radovljica has a small walled medieval core and the Beekeeping Museum. Slovenia is disproportionately, almost obsessively, a beekeeping country. Anton Janša (1734–1773), born just up the road, was appointed the first teacher of beekeeping at the Habsburg court by Maria Theresa and is regarded as the founder of modern apiculture. World Bee Day is 20 May because that is his birthday — a Slovenian proposal the UN adopted in 2017. The native Carniolan grey bee from these valleys is now one of the most widely kept subspecies on earth.

The museum's treasure is the painted beehive panels (panjske končnice) — the small front boards of hives, painted from the eighteenth century onward with saints, devils, hunting parties, and a recurring joke about hunters being buried by the animals. A folk art form that exists essentially nowhere else.

And France Prešeren was born at Vrba, a few minutes from camp. The seventh stanza of one of his poems is the Slovene national anthem — a toast wishing that all nations should live free and never make war on each other.

Heads up

Practical notes for the day.

  • Cobbles and rain. The setts get genuinely slippery wet. Brake before the corner, not in it.
  • Traffic. Vršič is a famous cycling climb and a motorcycling destination, and also carries coaches and campervans on a road with very few passing places. Far worse in the afternoon than the morning — the practical argument for going early, quite apart from the weather.
  • Charging. Essentially nothing between Kranjska Gora and Bovec. Go over full. You will claw back a useful amount on the descents but do not plan around it.
  • Signal. Patchy on the pass, effectively absent in upper Trenta. Download offline maps.
  • Cash. Huts, smaller places in Trenta and some parking are cash-preferred.
  • Park rules. You are inside Triglav National Park all day — no wild camping, no fires, no drones without a permit, stay on marked trails. Enforcement is real.
  • Unexploded ordnance. A century on, the front still throws things up. Extremely unlikely on a marked trail, but if you see corroded metal that could be a shell, do not touch it, note the location, report it. Standard advice throughout the region and not theoretical.

05Day 4 · Tue 11 AugKamna Gorica → the coast · ~6.5 h

The run south

Seven and a half hours across three landscapes. The interesting part is a wind.

On the ground

The A1 south of Zagreb is one of the more dramatic motorways in Europe and nobody talks about it. It crosses the Velebit, the long limestone wall that separates continental Croatia from the coast, through the Sveti Rok and Mala Kapela tunnels. You go in through green, hilly, central-European farmland and come out the other side into bare white karst, Mediterranean scrub and the sea. It is one of the most abrupt landscape transitions on the continent and it happens in about four kilometres of tunnel.

History

That white rock is not natural desert. The Velebit and much of the Dalmatian hinterland were forested until Venice cut them down — centuries of shipbuilding for the Arsenale stripped the slopes, the thin soil washed off the limestone, and it never came back. The bare karst you are looking at is a five-hundred-year-old industrial scar.

Local knowledge

The bura. The wind that comes down off the Velebit onto the Maslenica channel is a katabatic wind — cold dense air pooling on the plateau behind the range, then falling over the edge and accelerating under gravity. It arrives in violent, discrete gusts rather than a steady blow, and it is among the strongest recorded winds in Europe: gusts over 200 km/h have been logged on the Maslenica bridge, which is closed to high-sided vehicles and sometimes to all traffic when it blows. Croatians distinguish the bura (cold, dry, clears the sky) from the jugo (warm, humid, southerly, brings rain and, according to everyone, bad moods). In August you are unlikely to meet a serious bura, but the roadside wind-warning signs and the anchored guard rails are not decorative.

History

You cross a border that used to be the edge of the world, twice. Slovenia into Croatia is a Schengen internal frontier now — Croatia joined the Schengen area on 1 January 2023 and adopted the euro on the same day — so the booths at Bregana are usually waved through. It has not always been so casual. This line was the Militargrenze, the Habsburg Military Frontier: a militarised belt maintained for roughly three centuries as a permanent buffer against the Ottomans, where peasant-soldiers held land in exchange for lifelong military service. It is why the demographic map of this region is as complicated as it is — Vienna settled Orthodox Serb refugees along the frontier as border troops, and the consequences of that ran directly into the 1990s.

South of Karlovac you enter Lika, and if it feels empty, it is. Lika is one of the most depopulated regions in Europe — harsh karst, poor soil, a century of emigration, then the war. Whole villages along the old road are roofless. The motorway sails over it at 130 km/h and you would never know.

Local knowledge

The Maslenica bridge you cross was blown up. The original bridge over the Novsko Ždrilo channel was destroyed in November 1991, cutting Dalmatia off from the rest of Croatia by road entirely — for over a year the only land link to Split and Zadar was a pontoon bridge under artillery observation, and a ferry. The current motorway bridge dates from 1997 and the older road bridge was rebuilt alongside. It is the single most strategically important 300 metres of road in Croatia and there is nothing at all to mark it.

Tolls. The A1 is tolled by ticket — take one entering, pay leaving. Cards work at every plaza. Rotterdam to the coast, the Croatian leg alone runs to a meaningful sum, and the ENC electronic tag is only worth it for residents.

Heads up

Charging on the A1 is thinner than it looks on the map. The service areas (odmorišta) have chargers but the network is patchier and busier than anything you have driven so far this trip, and August is peak transit season for the entire German and Austrian holiday flow heading to Dalmatia. Charge before you need to rather than when you need to, and treat Karlovac and Zadar as the two reliable anchors. Once you turn off onto the coastal road toward Omiš the options thin further.

The other August factor is traffic itself. Saturdays are changeover day across the whole Adriatic coast and the A1 southbound can back up badly at the tunnels. A weekday run is a different road.

Local knowledge

What to say. Croatian and Slovene are close but not the same, and switching is noticed and appreciated. dobar dan good day · hvala thank you · molim please, and also “you’re welcome”, and also “pardon?” · živjeli cheers. In Slovenia it was dober dan and hvala — close enough that you will get away with the wrong one.

06Days 4–5 · 11–13 AugCamped at Lokva Rogoznica · Croatia

Brela and the Biokovo wall

A 1,762-metre mountain standing directly behind a beach, which is why the beach is white.

Heads up

One thing to correct in your own notes: Autocamp Sirena at Lokva Rogoznica is north of Brela, not south. Lokva Rogoznica sits between Omiš and Brela — roughly 20 minutes up the coast from Punta Rata, not down it. Your trip data has it as "~10 min south of Brela," which will send you the wrong way at a junction. The upside is that it puts Omiš and the Cetina gorge on your doorstep rather than Makarska — see below, because Omiš is the better story.

On the ground

The Makarska Riviera is a narrow shelf of pine and pebble beach pinned between the Adriatic and the Biokovo massif, whose summit Sveti Jure reaches 1,762 m barely four kilometres from the water. That vertical wall is why the strip has its own microclimate, why the light is so hard in the afternoon, and why the beaches are made of white limestone pebbles rather than sand — the material comes straight off the mountain.

Punta Rata at Brela is the beach that keeps appearing on "best in Europe" lists, and the reason is the Brela Stone (Brela kamen) — a lump of rock standing just offshore with a single pine tree growing out of it. It is the emblem of the whole Riviera and it is on every postcard from here to Split.

The water clarity is a karst effect. There are almost no rivers dumping sediment along this stretch — rain sinks straight into the limestone and travels underground — so the sea stays glass-clear. You will occasionally hit a cold patch swimming: that is a freshwater spring coming up through the seabed.

Local knowledge

Water shoes. Pebble beaches are hard on bare feet, and there are sea urchins on the rockier entries. Every local family has plastic sandals. Buy a pair at the first supermarket you see and stop thinking about it.

The Biokovo Skywalk is a glass horseshoe platform cantilevered over a 1,200 m drop, opened in 2020. Getting there means the Biokovo Nature Park road, which is narrow, single-track in sections, and operates a timed-entry system with a vehicle cap. book ahead; check EV suitability of the climb The road climbs over 1,200 m in about 20 km — it is a real drain on range, though you recover a lot coming back down.

History

Omiš, half an hour north where the Cetina river bursts out of a gorge, was a genuine pirate state. From the twelfth to the thirteenth century the Kačić clan ran fast, shallow-draft galleys called sagittae out of the river mouth and preyed on Venetian shipping — they beat off Venetian punitive fleets more than once and even took on a crusader convoy, which got them excommunicated. The gorge behind the town was the hideout: you cannot see into it from the sea. Venice eventually took the place in 1444 and the piracy stopped.

Local knowledge

Three Dalmatian things worth eating here rather than later:

  • Peka — meat or octopus with potatoes cooked under a bell-shaped iron lid buried in embers, for three hours. Almost always has to be ordered a day in advance. Worth organising on your first evening for your second.
  • Pašticada — beef larded, marinated in vinegar, then stewed slowly with prunes and prošek dessert wine, served with gnocchi. The great Dalmatian celebration dish.
  • Soparnik — a thin chard pie baked under embers, from the Poljica region just north of here, and EU protected-designation. Poljica was an autonomous peasant republic that governed itself under its own statute from the thirteenth century until Napoleon abolished it in 1807; there is a persistent local claim that Thomas More knew of it and used it as a model for Utopia. Treat that as a proud local tradition rather than settled scholarship — but it is a very good story to have in the car.
History

The Poljica Republic — the best story on this coast that nobody tells. In the hills directly behind your campsite, between the Cetina and the Adriatic, a peasant commune governed itself for something close to six hundred years. Poljica had a written statute from the fifteenth century, an elected head of state called the veliki knez who served a one-year term, twelve districts each with its own council, and a legal code covering property, inheritance and criminal law — all administered by farmers, not nobles.

It paid tribute to whoever was strongest at the time — Hungary, Venice, the Ottomans, Venice again — and in exchange was left to run itself. Napoleon abolished it in 1807, which is roughly when he abolished everything else around here. There is a persistent claim that Thomas More knew of Poljica and drew on it for Utopia; it is unprovable and probably too neat, but the fact that it is plausible tells you what kind of place this was.

The one thing everybody has eaten without knowing the connection is soparnik — a thin flat pie of chard, onion and oil baked between two sheets of dough directly on the hearth under embers. It is the dish of Poljica, it has protected designation status, and you will find it at markets and roadside stands around Omiš. Eat it.

On the ground

The Cetina is the reason to be based here rather than at Brela. The river comes out of the karst inland, runs through a limestone canyon, and reaches the sea at Omiš through a gorge so narrow the town is essentially wedged into it. The water is cold, green and fast, and it supports the entire adventure-sports economy of this stretch of coast:

  • Rafting — the standard commercial run is gentle by Tara standards, grade II–III, more scenery than adrenaline. Given the Tara is ahead of you, treat this as a swim with a boat attached rather than the main event.
  • Canyoning — the better call here. Wetsuits, jumps, natural slides, abseils down waterfalls. Half-day and full-day options run out of Omiš.
  • Zip-line — eight wires strung across the canyon, the longest around 700 m. Roughly three hours including the transfer up.
  • The fortresses — Mirabela sits above the town on the rock (short, steep, cheap) and Fortica is a proper 30–45 minute climb above it with the whole coast and the island of Brač laid out below. Go at the end of the day.
Local knowledge

Biokovo is a botanical island. The wall behind you is not just scenery — the 1,762 m rise straight off the sea creates a compressed sequence of climate zones, and the massif holds endemic plants found nowhere else. It also generates its own weather with some enthusiasm: it can be thirty degrees on the beach and blowing cold cloud over Sveti Jure.

If you do the Biokovo road, the Skywalk is the headline but the drive itself is the experience — and the view from the top on a clear day reaches to Italy. timed entry, vehicle cap, and gate closing time — book ahead and check the descent is open The descent is where an EV wins: expect to recover a serious chunk of what the climb costs you.

Klapa is the local music — unaccompanied male close-harmony singing, originally from Dalmatian church and tavern tradition, now on the UNESCO intangible heritage list. Omiš hosts the main festival of it each summer, and hearing it live in a stone courtyard is genuinely different from hearing it on a recording. festival dates vary year to year

Local knowledge

Eat and drink Dalmatian, not Croatian-generic. This coast has its own repertoire and the tourist strip will happily sell you pizza instead.

  • Peka (or ispod čepnje) — meat or octopus and potatoes cooked under an iron bell buried in embers. Must be ordered hours ahead, usually a day. Worth organising once.
  • Crni rižot — cuttlefish-ink risotto. Stains everything. Order it anyway.
  • Gregada — the old Dalmatian fish stew, white fish with potato, onion, white wine and oil. Fisherman food, rarely on tourist menus.
  • Paški sir — hard sheep cheese from the island of Pag, where the sheep graze on herbs salted by the bura. Distinctive and expensive.
  • Fritule and rozata — the doughnut and the custard. Rozata is Dubrovnik's, and better there.

To drink: Pošip (white, Korčula) and Plavac Mali (red, the Pelješac grape you meet properly tomorrow). Bevanda is red cut with still water, gemišt is white cut with sparkling — both entirely normal at lunch and not considered a crime. Rakija comes as loza (grape), šljivovica (plum), travarica (herb) and medica (honey); it will be offered free after a meal and refusing outright is mildly rude.

07Day 6 · Thu 13 AugPelješac · Ston · the Neum corridor

Why Bosnia has twenty kilometres of coast

The strangest border in Europe, and it is entirely Dubrovnik's fault.

History

Bosnia and Herzegovina is effectively landlocked except for a 20 km strip of coast at Neum, which splits Croatia in two. It looks like a Yugoslav-era accident. It is not — it is a deliberate piece of Ragusan diplomacy from 1699.

The Republic of Ragusa (Dubrovnik) had spent centuries balancing between Venice and the Ottomans. At the Treaty of Karlowitz, with Venice advancing down the coast, Ragusa gave away the Neum strip and the land at Sutorina in the south to the Ottoman Empire — on purpose. The point was to make sure Ragusa never shared a land border with Venice again. Two buffer zones, deliberately created, so that any Venetian army marching on Dubrovnik would first have to invade the Ottoman Empire. It worked. And the border survived the Ottomans, Austria-Hungary, two Yugoslavias and the 1990s wars essentially unchanged. A 327-year-old diplomatic firewall is still on your satnav.

On the ground

The Pelješac Bridge, opened in July 2022, is the modern answer: a 2,404 m cable-stayed bridge leaping the bay from the mainland to the Pelješac peninsula, letting you reach Dubrovnik entirely within Croatia and skip two border crossings. Take it. It is also a genuinely handsome piece of engineering and worth a look back at from the Pelješac side.

History

Ston, at the neck of the peninsula, has about five kilometres of defensive wall running up over the hills — one of the longest fortification systems in Europe, and the longest in Europe outside... well, it is the standard claim, and the walls are certainly extraordinary. Ragusa began them in 1333. They were not built to protect people. They were built to protect salt.

The Ston saltworks are among the oldest continuously operating in Europe, still worked by hand in shallow evaporation pans exactly as they were in the fourteenth century. Salt was the single most valuable commodity in the medieval Adriatic — it preserved fish and meat, which meant it fed armies and cities — and Ston salt at times generated a very large share of the Republic's revenue. Ragusa walled its salt pans the way a state walls a treasury, because that is what they were.

Local knowledge

Mali Ston oysters. The bay behind Ston has been farming the flat European oyster (Ostrea edulis) since Roman times. It works because the Neretva river's freshwater mixes with the sea here at just the right salinity. These are not the Pacific oysters you get everywhere — the native flat oyster is rarer, metallic, and much more interesting. Eaten at the quayside with lemon and a glass of Pošip.

Pelješac wine. The peninsula grows Plavac Mali, a grape that DNA testing eventually revealed to be a cross of Crljenak Kaštelanski (which turns out to be Zinfandel/Primitivo) and Dobričić. Two appellations matter: Dingač — Croatia's first protected wine region, designated 1961 — and Postup. Both grow on absurdly steep south-facing slopes dropping straight to the sea, where the vines get sun three times over: direct, reflected off the water, and radiated back off the white rock. Until 1973 the grapes came off those slopes by donkey; then they blasted the Dingač tunnel through the ridge so tractors could get in. Look for it as you drive the peninsula.

Local knowledge

Pelješac is the best red-wine peninsula in Croatia and you are driving its length. The grape is Plavac Mali — “little blue one” — and it is a natural crossing of Zinfandel (Crljenak Kaštelanski, DNA-confirmed at UC Davis in 2001 to be genetically the same as Primitivo and Zinfandel) with an old Dalmatian variety called Dobričić. So the wine you are about to drink is Zinfandel's Croatian child, made where its parent came from.

Two named zones matter, both on the steep south-facing slopes dropping to the sea:

  • Dingač — the first protected appellation in Croatia (1961). Vines on gradients so severe that the fruit is worked by hand and was historically brought out by donkey; a tunnel through the ridge was cut in the 1970s so the grapes could reach the cellars at all. Full, high-alcohol, sun-baked reds. Expensive for a reason.
  • Postup — the neighbouring appellation, slightly less extreme, often better value.

Cellars along the Potomje–Dingač road do tastings. Given the driving, this is a buy-and-carry stop rather than a session — and note that Bosnia and Montenegro both have their own duty allowances on the crossings ahead.

On the ground

Ston is worth the stop for forty-five minutes, not for two hours. Walk a section of the wall rather than the whole circuit — the climb from Ston up toward the Podzvizd tower gives you the salt pans, the bay and the wall snaking over the hill in one frame, and it takes about twenty minutes each way. In full August sun there is no shade on it whatever.

The salt pans below are still worked by hand. Harvest runs through the summer and you may see it happening. Bags of Ston salt are sold on site and make the single best-value souvenir on this coast.

Local knowledge

Mali Ston oysters, and when not to eat them. The flat European oyster farmed in this bay is the same species the Romans prized, and the traditional feast day is 19 March, St Joseph's — which tells you the season. The old rule about months with an R in them is about spawning, not safety: through the warm months the oyster puts its energy into reproduction and the meat goes milky and soft. August is not their best month. They are still good, and the setting — eaten at a table over the water where they are grown — does a lot of work. Mussels from the same bay are excellent year-round and cheaper.

Heads up

The Pelješac Bridge does not remove every border question. It removes the two Neum crossings, which is the point, and you should take it. But if you end up on the old coastal road for any reason — diversion, curiosity, a wrong turn — you will pass through Bosnian territory twice in twenty kilometres, and that means passports out, EU pet and vehicle documents relevant, and potentially long queues in August. There is no meaningful reason to do this deliberately.

Green card. Your insurance situation changes at the Montenegro and Bosnia crossings later in the trip — worth confirming your policy covers both and carrying the physical green card rather than relying on a phone. check your insurer's country list for BiH and Montenegro before the Kotor leg

08Day 7 · Fri 14 AugDubrovnik at dawn · Croatia

Dubrovnik

A city-state that survived 450 years by never once winning a war.

Non bene pro toto libertas venditur auro.
Liberty is not well sold for all the gold. Carved over the entrance to Fort Lovrijenac
History

The Republic of Ragusa ran itself as an independent state from roughly 1358 to 1808, wedged between Venice, the Ottomans and Habsburg Hungary, with a population you could fit in a large football stadium. It survived by being useful to everyone and threatening to no one. From 1458 it paid an annual tribute to the Sultan — and in exchange got trading privileges across the entire Ottoman Balkans, which no Christian rival had. Ragusan merchants had colonies in Sofia, Belgrade and Constantinople. The tribute was not weakness; it was the business model.

The constitution was paranoid about tyranny in a way that is almost funny. The Rector, the head of state, served a term of one month. During it he had to live in the Rector's Palace and could barely leave. He was decorative — real power sat with the Great Council of nobles. No one man could accumulate anything.

Two more Ragusan firsts worth carrying with you:

  • Quarantine was invented here. In 1377 Ragusa ordered ships and caravans from plague areas to be isolated for thirty days on nearby islands before entering — trentino. It was later extended to forty days, quarantena. That is where the word comes from. The Lazareti complex just outside Ploče Gate is the surviving purpose-built quarantine station.
  • The slave trade was abolished by the Republic in 1416, centuries ahead of its neighbours.

The Franciscan monastery pharmacy off the Stradun has been operating since 1317 and is one of the oldest continuously working pharmacies in Europe. It still dispenses.

On the ground

The Stradun was a sea channel. There were originally two settlements — a Roman-refugee town on a rocky islet called Laus (which becomes Ragusa) and a Slavic settlement in the oak woods on the mainland shore (dubrava → Dubrovnik). The channel between them was filled in during the twelfth century. The main street is the old strait, which is why it is dead straight and why the city has two names.

Why it all looks the same age. The great earthquake of 1667 killed several thousand people and flattened most of the city. What you walk through is the disciplined, uniform Baroque rebuild — the authorities imposed a standardised design on the whole main street. The Sponza Palace and the outer walls are among the few significant survivors of the older city.

The roofs. Look at the tiles from the walls: a patchwork of faded terracotta and bright new orange. The bright ones are replacements from after the 1991–92 siege, when the JNA shelled the old town from Srđ and the sea. There is a map inside the Pile Gate showing every recorded shell strike. It is worth two minutes.

Local knowledge

The dawn plan is the right plan. The wall circuit is roughly 2 km, has almost no shade, and is walked in a compulsory anticlockwise direction — meaning if you get stuck behind a tour group you stay stuck. Cruise ship passengers arrive mid-morning. Being on the walls at opening, before the heat and before the ships, is the difference between one of the great city walks in Europe and a queue on a hot wall. check current opening time

Fort Lovrijenac, on its own rock outside the walls, is often included with the walls ticket and is far less crowded. It has the best view back at the city. It is also the Red Keep, if that matters to anyone in the car; the Jesuit Stairs were the walk of shame.

Srđ, the mountain behind, has a cable car and a road. The fort on top — Fort Imperial — was built by Napoleon's occupying forces between 1806 and 1816, and in 1991 a small Croatian force held it against the JNA in a siege-within-a-siege. There is a Homeland War museum inside. Sunset from up there is the other great Dubrovnik view and almost nobody on a day trip does it.

On the ground

Dawn is not a preference here, it is the only sane strategy. Dubrovnik receives cruise passengers in blocks — several thousand people can come ashore inside a two-hour window, and they funnel through two gates into a walled area you can cross in ten minutes. The city has been capping daily cruise arrivals and limiting how many ships berth simultaneously precisely because the old town was becoming unwalkable. current cruise-cap arrangements and wall opening times

Practically: be at Pile Gate when the walls open. You get the Stradun empty, the light low and gold on the limestone, and the whole circuit before the heat. By eleven the walls are a queue with a view, and the stone — which is polished, pale and reflective — becomes genuinely punishing. There is almost no shade on the circuit and the exit points are limited once you commit.

The walls run about two kilometres and take 1.5–2 hours at a photographing pace. Go anticlockwise from Pile: it puts the hard climbing early and the sea section, which is the good half, in better light.

History

The siege is recent and it is legible if you look up. From October 1991 the city was shelled from the ridge of Srđ above it and from the sea, through a siege that lasted around seven months. The old town — a UNESCO site, of no military value — took direct hits on a scale that caused international outrage and eventually contributed to war-crimes convictions.

The evidence is on the roofs. Look at the tiles from any high point: the bright orange ones are post-1991 replacements and the faded, lichened, mismatched ones are what survived. The proportion of new to old is a damage map of the shelling, and once you have seen it you cannot look at the skyline any other way. There is also a marble plaque near the Pile Gate mapping every strike on the old town.

On Srđ, the hill above, the Napoleonic Fort Imperial held out through the siege with a tiny garrison and now houses a small, unvarnished museum about the Homeland War. The cable car runs up; the walking route is steep, exposed and best done very early or not at all in August.

Local knowledge

Things inside the walls worth ten minutes each.

  • The Onofrio Fountain at the Pile end — the terminus of a 12 km aqueduct completed in 1438, which is why Ragusa could withstand siege. Drinkable, cold, free. Fill your bottles.
  • The Franciscan pharmacy — working since 1317, one of the oldest in Europe, still dispensing.
  • Fort Lovrijenac — outside the walls on its own rock, with the liberty inscription over the door. The Republic built it in a hurry, reputedly in three months, to stop Venice building there first.
  • The Rector's Palace — go to understand the one-month term. The building is designed to contain a head of state, not to glorify one.
  • Buza — literally “hole in the wall”. A gap in the seaward wall opening onto cliff terraces with a bar and swimming off the rocks. Cash, sunset, and it is exactly as good as it sounds.
Local knowledge

King's Landing. Much of Game of Thrones was shot here and the city has an entire economy built on it. The Jesuit Stairs are the walk of shame; Lovrijenac and the Bokar fortress are the Red Keep exteriors; Trsteno Arboretum up the coast is the palace gardens. Whether that is a draw or a reason to keep walking is your call, but it explains the crowds at specific, otherwise unremarkable staircases.

Lokrum, the island fifteen minutes out by boat, is the good escape — botanical gardens, a ruined Benedictine monastery, peacocks introduced by Maximilian of Habsburg, and a small salt lake to swim in. There is also a curse: the monks, expelled in 1808, are said to have cursed anyone who took the island for themselves, and locals cheerfully list the subsequent owners who died badly. No one stays overnight.

Heads up

Do not drive into Dubrovnik. Parking near the old town is expensive and scarce even at dawn, and the approach roads clog. Park at one of the outlying garages or use the campsite–bus combination and walk in. If you are arriving before the buses run, aim for a garage on the Ploče side and accept the cost.

Dress code. The cathedral and the Franciscan and Dominican monasteries enforce covered shoulders and knees. In August with a dawn start this catches people out.

09Days 7–9 · 14–17 AugBay of Kotor · 3 nights, Dobrota

The Boka

Not a fjord. A drowned river canyon, with a Venetian city at the bottom of it.

On the ground

You will read everywhere that the Bay of Kotor is "the southernmost fjord in Europe." It is not a fjord and never was. A fjord is glacially carved. The Boka is a ria — a river canyon cut by water, then drowned when sea levels rose after the last ice age. The river that carved it flowed roughly where the bay now bends. Knowing this actually improves the view: you are looking at a canyon with the sea poured into it.

The bay is nearly closed off at the Verige strait, only a few hundred metres wide, which is why it was such a coveted anchorage — a fleet inside it is invisible and unreachable. The Kamenari–Lepetane ferry crosses there in about ten minutes and cuts a substantial loop off the drive around the inner bay. Runs frequently, pay on board. confirm it takes EVs without restriction

History

Kotor's layers, in order: Roman Acruvium; Byzantine; then part of the Serbian Nemanjić kingdom, which is when it grew rich on trade with the Balkan interior; then Venetian from 1420 to 1797, as Cattaro, for 377 years — which is why everything you are looking at is Venetian. Then Habsburg, briefly Napoleonic, Austro-Hungarian, Italian-occupied in the Second World War, then Yugoslav.

St Tryphon's Cathedral was consecrated in 1166 and is one of the oldest cathedrals in the region. The relics arrived in 809, when a Kotor merchant bought the saint's remains from Venetian traders — the same acquisitive relic economy that got Venice the body of St Mark. Tryphon has been the city's patron ever since.

The Boka Navy (Bokeljska mornarica) traces itself to that same year, 809, which would make it one of the oldest continuously existing maritime guilds in the world. It survives today as a ceremonial fraternity with its own uniform and its own circular sword dance, performed on the feast of St Tryphon in February.

In 1979 an earthquake devastated the whole Montenegrin coast and did enormous damage to Kotor. The restoration that followed is a large part of why the town is a UNESCO site.

On the ground

The fortress walls. The fortifications run 4.5 km and climb the cliff behind the town to San Giovanni (Sveti Ivan) fortress at about 260 m, up roughly 1,350 steps. It is a hard, exposed, hot climb and the reason to do it at first light or in the last hour of daylight, with water. The payoff — the whole bay laid out beneath you, the town a stone toy below — is the single best thing in Montenegro.

The alternative that locals use: the Ladder of Kotor (Kotorske Skale / the Kotor Serpentine), an old caravan trail of seventy-odd switchbacks that climbed from the bay up to the Krstac pass, long before there was a road. It is the historic route to Cetinje and it is free. There is also long-standing local knowledge about a hole in the wall near the Church of Our Lady of Remedy that lets you join the fortress path without a ticket. access has been fenced and reopened at various times — check locally, don't plan on it

The cats. Kotor is full of them, deliberately. The story is that sailors valued cats on ships and left them here; the town adopted them, there is a small cat museum, and a great deal of merchandise. They are healthy, fed by locals, and they own the place.

Local knowledge

Montenegrin food is where the coast and the mountains meet on one plate. Look for njeguški pršut (smoked ham from the village of Njeguši, up on Lovćen — see below), kajmak (a clotted, salted dairy spread that goes on everything), crni rižoto (cuttlefish-ink risotto), and anything na buzaru — shellfish in white wine, garlic and breadcrumbs.

The wine to drink is Vranac (red, dark, tannic — the name means "black stallion") and Krstač (white). Most of it comes from Plantaže near Podgorica, whose main cellar, Šipčanik, is a converted underground aircraft hangar built to hide Yugoslav air force jets. It holds a couple of million litres in a tunnel designed to survive an air strike.

History

Why the Boka is Catholic and Orthodox at the same time. Walk Kotor and you will pass a Catholic cathedral and an Orthodox church within a hundred metres of each other, and this is not modern tolerance — it is the residue of four centuries of Venice governing a Slavic Orthodox population and needing them to keep trading. The bay sat on the seam between the Venetian maritime world and the Ottoman interior, and it made its money by being the door between them. Kotor merchants sold Balkan wool, hides, wax and silver into the Adriatic and shipped Venetian manufactures back inland. A city that lives on a border learns to be flexible about what its neighbours believe.

That is also why the architecture reads Italian. The stone, the campaniles, the winged lion of St Mark over the Sea Gate, the piazza layout — all Venetian. The language spoken under it never was.

On the ground

The fortress climb is the thing to do, and the hour you do it decides whether you enjoy it. The walls run from the town up to the fortress of St John (San Giovanni) at around 260 m, roughly 1,350 steps, switchbacking up the cliff behind the old town. It takes 45–90 minutes up depending on stops and heat.

In August, go at first light or after five. The stone is uneven, polished by centuries of feet, and there is no shade on the middle section. Take more water than you think — there is a small stand partway but do not rely on it. The Church of Our Lady of Remedy sits halfway up and makes a natural breather.

There is a well-known informal back route via the “Ladder of Kotor” and a gap in the wall that people use to avoid the entry fee. It is rougher, unmarked in places and involves loose ground. The fee is modest; pay it.

Local knowledge

The cats. Kotor is full of them, they are semi-officially the town's mascot, and there is a small museum devoted to them. The usual explanation is that sailors valued cats on ships for rats and the population simply persisted. They are fed and looked after by the town. Do not be surprised to find one asleep on your restaurant chair.

Where to base yourself in the evening. You are staying at Dobrota, just up the bay, which is the right call — the old town empties of day-trippers and cruise passengers by early evening and becomes something else entirely. The waterfront promenade from Dobrota into Kotor is flat and walkable and takes about half an hour.

Eat: njeguški pršut and cheese to start, crni rižot, grilled fish sold by the kilo (ask the price before it is cooked, this is where tourist bills go wrong), and priganice — fried dough with honey and cheese — for breakfast. The local mussels, bužara style in white wine and garlic, are farmed in the bay and excellent.

Heads up

Montenegro is not in the EU or Schengen but does use the euro — unilaterally, without a currency agreement. Cards are widely accepted in Kotor and much less so once you go inland. Draw cash before Žabljak.

Driving in the bay is the real difficulty. The coastal road round the inner bay is narrow, two-way, lined with parked cars and busy. The Kamenari–Lepetane ferry across the Verige strait cuts a substantial loop and costs little. confirm the ferry takes EVs without restriction and check current fares

Cruise days. When two large ships are alongside, the old town is genuinely unpleasant between ten and four. Plan the fortress and the town for early morning and use the middle of the day for the water or the drive.

10Day 9 · Sun 16 AugPerast · Risan · Herceg Novi

Perast

A village of four hundred people that trained the Russian navy and built its own island.

History

Perast has sixteen Baroque palaces and seventeen churches for a population that has never been large. It got rich as Venice's most reliable local ally — Perast held the honour of guarding the Venetian war standard, and its captains were among the best in the Adriatic.

In 1698 Peter the Great sent Russian noblemen to Perast to learn seamanship, at the nautical school of Captain Marko Martinović. Russia had almost no maritime tradition; Peter was building one from nothing, and he sent his cadets to a village in the Bay of Kotor. There is a plaque. It is a genuinely astonishing footnote for a place this size.

History

Our Lady of the Rocks (Gospa od Škrpjela) is an artificial island, and its construction method is the best story in the bay. In 1452, sailors are said to have found an icon of the Madonna on a rock barely breaking the surface. They began a tradition: every time they returned safely from a voyage, they dropped a stone on the reef. They also deliberately sank captured and worn-out ships loaded with rocks around it. Over centuries the reef became an island, and they built a church on it.

The tradition is not historical — it is current. Every 22 July, at sunset, the Fašinada takes place: a procession of boats rows out with stones and drops them around the island, exactly as it has been done for five hundred years, to keep widening it. It is men only, rowed, and accompanied by singing. You will miss it by weeks, but the island you are standing on is still growing.

Inside, look for the silver votive plaques left by sailors and the embroidered icon made by Jacinta Kunić-Mijović, who worked on it for twenty-five years while waiting for a sailor who never came back, using her own hair in the thread. You can see the hair change colour from dark to grey across the piece.

History

Risan, five minutes further round the bay, is the oldest settlement in the Boka and almost nobody stops. It was the Illyrian capital, and it is where Queen Teuta took refuge around 229 BC after the Romans came for her — she had been licensing piracy against Roman shipping, and when Rome sent envoys to complain she had one of them killed, which triggered the First Illyrian War and Rome's first serious involvement on this coast. Everything Roman, Venetian and Ottoman that follows on this trip starts, in a sense, with that.

What survives is better than the story deserves: a set of Roman floor mosaics from a 2nd-century villa, including one of Hypnos, god of sleep, reclining — believed to be the only known mosaic depiction of him in situ anywhere. It is under a modest shelter in an unremarkable yard, costs very little, and takes fifteen minutes.

History

Herceg Novi, at the mouth of the bay, is the odd one out on this coast: it was founded in 1382 by Tvrtko I, King of Bosnia — not by Rome, not by Venice. He wanted a port of his own and built one. It was then taken by the Ottomans, briefly by Spain, by Venice, by Russia, by France and by Austria, and each left a fort. Kanli Kula — Ottoman, and the name means Bloody Tower for what went on in it — is now an open-air theatre. Forte Mare sits on the water; Španjola is on the hill above, from the Spanish year.

The town is built up a slope and is locally known as the city of a thousand steps, which is not a figure of speech. It is also the botanical one — nineteenth-century sea captains brought back plants from everywhere they sailed, and the gardens are full of things that have no business being at this latitude.

On the ground

The neighbouring island, Sveti Đorđe, is natural — a real islet with a Benedictine abbey and a cypress cemetery. It is closed to visitors. It is the one in every photograph, because it is the prettier of the two.

Perast has no through-traffic in the centre; you park at either end and walk. Small boats run out to the island constantly from the waterfront, take a few minutes, and cost very little.

On the ground

How the boats work. Small boats run to Our Lady of the Rocks continuously from the Perast waterfront — a five-minute crossing, cheap, paid in cash, no need to book. They wait while you visit. There is no reason to buy a longer organised tour unless you want the Blue Cave as well.

Perast has no through traffic — cars are stopped at either end and you park on the approach road and walk in. This is why it feels different from everywhere else on the bay. Expect a short walk and a parking fee.

The bell tower of St Nicholas is climbable, and the view straight down onto the two islands is the shot. Small fee, steep wooden stairs, worth it.

Swimming off the Perast waterfront is deep, clean and cold — the bay is fed by karst springs. There is a small municipal beach and several jetties.

History

The other island is the argument. Our Lady of the Rocks is artificial; St George, the cypress-covered one next to it, is natural, and holds a Benedictine abbey documented from the twelfth century along with the old Perast cemetery. The two islands are a neat summary of the bay's politics — St George was under the abbot and, through him, Venetian and Kotoran authority, while Our Lady of the Rocks was built and controlled by the people of Perast themselves. Building your own island is a way of not asking permission.

1654. Perast's defining moment is a battle in which a few hundred Perastani held off a substantially larger Ottoman force attacking the town. The victory is still commemorated, and it is the source of the town's standing with Venice.

Gadanje kokota — every 15 May, Perast men row out and shoot at a cockerel tied to a floating target, commemorating the expulsion of the Ottomans. The winner gets a scarf embroidered by the town's women. Yes, really.

History

Queen Teuta, and the pretext for Rome's first move east. Risan was the capital of the Illyrian Ardiaei, and Teuta ruled it as regent around 231–228 BC. Illyrian ships raided Adriatic shipping — piracy from the Roman view, licensed enterprise from hers. When Rome sent envoys to complain, the account passed down (from Polybius, writing later and not sympathetically) has her replying that it was not the custom of Illyrian rulers to interfere with private enterprise at sea, and one of the envoys being killed on the way home.

Rome invaded in 229 BC. It was the first Roman military action across the Adriatic, and it opened the road to everything that followed in the Balkans. Teuta was reduced to a rump territory around Risan and disappears from the record. There is a legend she threw herself from the cliffs; there is no evidence for it.

The Roman mosaics at Risan are the reason to stop — a second-century villa floor including a depiction of Hypnos, god of sleep, said to be the only such mosaic in the world. It is under a modest shelter, costs almost nothing, and is usually empty.

Local knowledge

Herceg Novi is the least Venetian town on the bay and the most layered — founded in 1382 by the Bosnian king Tvrtko I, then Ottoman for two centuries, then Venetian, then Austrian. Each left a fort, and they are all still there: the Ottoman Kanli Kula (“bloody tower”, a prison, now an open-air theatre), the Venetian Forte Mare on the water, and the Spanjola above. The town is built on stairs — hundreds of them — and it has a mimosa festival in February because the microclimate at the mouth of the bay is the mildest in Montenegro.

Just outside is Sutorina, the second Ragusan buffer strip mentioned earlier — the southern twin of Neum. Montenegro and Bosnia only formally settled that border in 2015.

11Day 8 · Sat 15 AugLovćen · Njeguši · Cetinje

Twenty-five hairpins to a poet's tomb

The old road out of Kotor, and the mountain that gave Montenegro its name and its stubbornness.

On the ground

The old Kotor–Cetinje road (the P1, the Kotor Serpentine) climbs the wall behind the bay in twenty-five numbered hairpins built by the Austro-Hungarians. It is narrow, largely without barriers, and used by tour buses coming the other way. It also gives the single most famous view in Montenegro, from the bend where the road doubles back in an "M" shape above the bay.

Drive it uphill from Kotor rather than down: on the inside of the hairpins you get the wall rather than the drop, and oncoming buses have to yield to the climbing vehicle. For an EV, the descent afterwards gives back a serious amount of the climb.

Local knowledge

Njeguši, at the top, is the ancestral village of the Petrović-Njegoš dynasty and the source of njeguški pršut — ham smoked over beech in the specific microclimate where cold mountain air meets sea air coming up the slope. It is served with the local hard cheese in oil and a shot of rakija at the roadside houses on the pass. This is the single most Montenegrin thing you can eat, and you are eating it at the source.

History

Petar II Petrović-Njegoš (1813–1851) is the figure to know. He was simultaneously Montenegro's prince and its Orthodox bishop — a vladika, ruling a theocratic principality where power passed uncle to nephew, because bishops could not have sons. He was reportedly close to two metres tall, he wrote The Mountain Wreath, still the central text of Montenegrin and Serbian literature, and he dragged a clan-based mountain society towards being a state.

His mausoleum sits on Jezerski Vrh at 1,657 m, reached by 461 steps through a tunnel. It was designed by Ivan Meštrović, the great Croatian sculptor, and it replaced Njegoš's original small chapel — a decision that was politically contentious at the time and still is in some quarters. From the terrace behind it you can see most of Montenegro and, on a clear day, into Albania, Croatia and Bosnia.

Cetinje, below, was the old royal capital: a small town of low buildings that nonetheless contains the former embassies of Russia, Britain, France, Italy and Austria-Hungary, all built when the Great Powers took Montenegro seriously as a Balkan piece on the board. Cetinje Monastery holds two of Orthodoxy's most significant relics — a fragment said to be of the True Cross, and the mummified right hand of St John the Baptist, which travelled from Constantinople via the Knights of Malta and the Russian imperial family before ending up here.

History

Why "Montenegro" — Crna Gora, black mountain — matters: this is one of the few parts of the Balkans the Ottomans never fully subdued. The terrain you are driving through is the reason. Clan warfare, blood feud and near-permanent low-level resistance from these mountains gave Montenegro a self-image as the Balkans' unconquered redoubt, and Njegoš is the man who wrote that self-image into literature.

Heads up

The serpentine is the most demanding driving of the trip so far. Twenty-five numbered hairpins, single-track in places with passing bays, low or absent parapets, and a drop that is genuinely vertical. Tour buses come down it and they cannot reverse. The convention is that the vehicle going up has right of way, which is one reason to drive it in that direction, but do not rely on a bus knowing that.

Go early. By mid-morning the buses are running and the famous “M” bend has cars stopped on it for photographs, which is exactly where you least want a stationary obstacle. There is a pull-off just above bend 17 that is safer for stopping than the ones people actually use.

Range: Kotor sits at sea level, Jezerski Vrh at 1,657 m. That is a serious climb. You will get a great deal of it back on the descent — but if you continue north to Cetinje and beyond rather than dropping back to the bay, you do not, so plan accordingly.

History

Why Montenegro was never really conquered. The Ottomans took the coast, took the plains, took Serbia and Bosnia outright — and never fully subdued the mountains behind you. Not because they could not have, but because the terrain made it more expensive than it was worth. The result was a society organised into clans (plemena), governed by customary law, with a blood-feud tradition and near-universal armament, that spent three centuries as an armed irritant on the empire's flank.

That is what Njegoš inherited and what he was trying to change: from clan confederation to state. He introduced the first taxes, the first court, the first standing guard. He was resisted at every step by people who saw all three as foreign impositions.

A note on The Mountain Wreath. It is a magnificent poem and it is also a problem. Its central action celebrates the istraga poturica — the extermination or expulsion of Montenegrins who had converted to Islam. Whether that event happened as described is disputed by historians; that the poem glorifies it is not. It was read approvingly by people doing terrible things in the 1990s. Slovene, Croat, Bosniak and Serb readers do not all encounter this text the same way, and it is worth knowing that before admiring it out loud in the wrong room.

Local knowledge

Njeguši is a working village, not a service station. The pršut is air-dried and beech-smoked in an unusually specific microclimate — cold dry mountain air meeting humid air rising off the bay — and the producers will tell you the combination cannot be reproduced elsewhere, which is the same claim made in every good ham region in Europe and is probably true in all of them. Buy a vacuum-packed piece; it travels.

Alongside it: njeguški sir, cheese matured in oil, and kačamak or cicvara — cornmeal and potato beaten with kajmak and cheese until it is more or less molten. Mountain food, enormous, and the correct order if you have been walking.

Cetinje repays an hour. The old royal capital was the diplomatic centre of a country of a few hundred thousand people, and the former embassies of Russia, Britain, France, Italy and Austria-Hungary are all still standing on one small grid of streets — now schools, offices and a music academy. King Nikola's palace is a modest house full of gifts from cousins who ran empires; he married his daughters into the Russian, Italian and Serbian royal houses and was known as “the father-in-law of Europe”. The Cetinje Monastery holds relics including what is venerated as the right hand of John the Baptist.

12Day 8 · Sat 15 AugSveti Stefan · the Budva riviera

Sveti Stefan

A fishing village that became a communist state hotel and then a five-star resort. You are here for the photograph.

History

The islet was fortified in the fifteenth century by local families as a refuge from Ottoman raids — the tradition is that it was paid for with loot taken from a Turkish ship. It stayed a working fishing village of a few dozen houses into the twentieth century.

Then, in the 1950s, Yugoslavia did something unusual: it moved the residents out and turned the entire village into a single hotel — a "town-hotel," where each old stone house became a suite. It became the glamour address of the socialist Adriatic, and the guest list ran to Sophia Loren, Elizabeth Taylor, Kirk Douglas and Princess Margaret. The idea of a state-run luxury resort inside a socialist country is one of the better illustrations of how differently Yugoslavia ran things.

Heads up

It is now leased as a private resort and the islet is not open to walk-in visitors. What everyone actually does — and what is genuinely worth doing — is view it from the mainland: the roadside viewpoints on the coastal highway above, the Miločer park path, or the public half of the beach on the causeway. That is a free, ten-minute, excellent stop. Going on the island is a resort booking in an entirely different price bracket. Worth being clear which one you mean before you set off.

Note also the resort's operating status has changed more than once in recent years. check what is actually open

On the ground

Budva is next door and you have already decided against it, correctly for your purposes — it is the loudest, most developed stretch of the Montenegrin coast. Worth knowing anyway that Budva's old town is genuinely ancient (Greek and Illyrian origins, Roman, then Venetian) and was almost entirely rebuilt after the 1979 earthquake. If you end up passing through, the walled core takes twenty minutes and the rest can be ignored.

Local knowledge

Where to actually stand. The classic elevated shot is from the pull-offs on the Adriatic highway above the village, looking down the length of the causeway. There is limited space and it is busy; the smaller lay-by slightly south usually has room. For the low-angle view, walk the Miločer park path — a shaded promenade through old pines and olives that runs from Miločer beach round to the causeway, ten minutes, free, and by some distance the nicest way to see it.

The park was the summer residence of the Karadđorđević royal family before the war. Queen Marija's beach, Kraljic̍ina plaža, is in the same stretch and access has been contested between public-access rules and resort leasing for years. which beaches here are currently public

History

The wider point about socialist Yugoslavia and tourism. Sveti Stefan is not an anomaly — it is the showpiece of a deliberate policy. After the 1948 split with Stalin, Yugoslavia needed hard currency and had no Soviet bloc to lean on. Tourism became a strategic industry: Yugoslav citizens could travel freely, Westerners could come in without much difficulty, and the Adriatic was built up through the 1960s and 70s with a distinctive modernist hotel architecture that is now being rediscovered and, in places, demolished.

It is why the Yugoslav experience of socialism was so different from the Eastern bloc's, and why nostalgia for it (Yugonostalgija) is a real and complicated thing you will encounter across every country on this trip.

On the ground

If you want a swim on this stretch rather than a photograph, the beaches immediately around Sveti Stefan are pebble and often crowded. Pržno, the small fishing cove just north, has a handful of konobas serving fish straight off the boats and is a better lunch than anything at the viewpoints.

The whole Budva riviera between here and Kotor is roughly forty minutes of coastal road with heavy August traffic. Budget more time than the distance suggests.

13Day 10 · Mon 17 AugKotor → Žabljak · ~3 h

Into the mountains

A monastery glued to a cliff, a brewery from 1896, and the last reliable charging for a while.

History

Ostrog Monastery is built into a vertical white cliff face — not against it, into it, with the church façades filling natural caves so the rock forms the walls. It was founded in the seventeenth century by Vasilije, Metropolitan of Herzegovina, who fled here from Ottoman pressure; he was canonised as St Basil of Ostrog and his relics are in the upper monastery.

What makes Ostrog unusual is who visits. It is the most important pilgrimage site in Montenegro for Orthodox Christians — but it is also visited by Catholics and by Muslims, who come to ask St Basil for healing. In a region defined by religious division, it is one of the few places where the division genuinely thins out. Some pilgrims walk the last stretch barefoot.

Dress modestly — covered shoulders and knees — and expect a crowd and a narrow, exposed access road up to the upper level.

On the ground

Nikšić is Montenegro's second city, an industrial town with a steelworks and a brewery. Nikšićko pivo, brewed here since 1896, is the national beer and the standard order everywhere in the country. Nikšić is also a practical waypoint: it is the last place of real size before the road quality and the charging network both thin out.

Heads up

This is the point where the EV plan stops being theoretical. From here to Sarajevo you are working with what is actually installed, not what a route planner promises. Leave Nikšić with a genuine buffer, not a calculated one.

On the ground

Ostrog is two monasteries, and the difference matters. The Lower Monastery is at the road, has parking, and is where most of the coaches stop. The Upper Monastery — the one glued into the cliff, the one in every photograph — is a further climb up a narrow, steep, switchbacking road with very limited parking at the top. In August it is often full, and cars queue on a road with nowhere to turn.

The pilgrim path between lower and upper takes about 30–40 minutes on foot through the woods. Some walk it barefoot as a penance. If the road looks blocked, park at the bottom and walk — it will genuinely be faster and it is the more interesting arrival.

Inside, the cave churches are tiny and the queue to the relics of St Basil moves slowly. Covered shoulders and knees, no shorts, and photography is restricted inside. There is no charge; donations are usual.

History

Vasilije of Ostrog retreated here in the 1660s from Ottoman pressure in Herzegovina and lived the rest of his life in the caves with a small community, carving out the churches. He was canonised soon after his death and the site became a pilgrimage centre almost immediately. The frescoes are painted directly onto the rock — there is no wall behind them.

The cross-confessional pilgrimage is not a modern tourist-board line. Muslims and Catholics have come to Ostrog for healing for centuries, and there is a documented history of Ottoman-era Muslims making the journey. In a region where identity has been organised around religious difference for six hundred years and was violently re-sorted twice in the twentieth century, that is worth noticing.

Heads up

This is where the EV plan meets reality, and it deserves a paragraph rather than a line.

  • Charge to full at Nikšić even if the numbers say you do not need to. It is the last town of size, and what is installed beyond it is sparse, sometimes broken, and often occupied.
  • The road to Žabljak climbs from around 600 m to 1,450 m, and Žabljak is the highest town in the Balkans. Sustained climbing at altitude in heat is the worst case for consumption. Budget generously.
  • The Đurđevića Tara bridge route and the Nikšić–Šavnik route differ considerably in surface quality and gradient. Check what your navigation has actually chosen and look at it on satellite before committing.
  • Carry your CEE adapters. In this part of Montenegro, a campsite or guesthouse socket is a realistic fallback and sometimes the only one. Ask the accommodation before you arrive rather than after.
  • Mobile coverage between Nikšić and Žabljak is patchy. Download the offline map.
Local knowledge

Nikšić is worth twenty minutes if you need a break. The brewery has been running since 1896 and Nikšićko is what you will be handed anywhere in the country. The town also has a genuine Roman bridge over the Zeta, the Tsar's Bridge, and an Ottoman fortress core — but it is fundamentally a socialist industrial city built around a steelworks, and it is the clearest look you will get on this trip at the Yugoslavia that was built rather than the Yugoslavia that was inherited.

North of it the landscape changes fast: the karst polje opens out, the tree line drops, and by the time you reach the Durmitor plateau you are somewhere that feels closer to Scandinavia than to the Adriatic you left three hours earlier.

14Days 10–12 · 17–20 AugDurmitor · Žabljak · 3 nights

Durmitor and the Tara canyon

Forty-eight peaks over two thousand metres, eighteen glacial lakes, and a bridge the engineer blew up himself.

On the ground

Durmitor is a limestone massif with forty-eight peaks above 2,000 m, topped by Bobotov Kuk at 2,523 m. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site and the geology is textbook glaciated karst: the eighteen lakes scattered through it are glacial, and Montenegrins call them gorske oči — mountain eyes.

Žabljak, the base town at 1,456 m, is the highest town in the Balkans. It is cold at night even in August — genuinely, pack a layer.

  • Black Lake (Crno jezero) — the big one, a 20-minute walk from town, with an easy, flat, beautiful loop of about 3.5 km around it. Two lakes joined by a channel that dries in late summer, at which point they separate. The Meded ("bear") peak stands directly over it.
  • Ćurevac — a viewpoint on the rim looking straight down into the Tara canyon.
  • Sedlo Pass (1,907 m) — the road across the western side of the massif, and one of the great drives in the country: bare limestone, no trees, sheer walls.
History

The Đurđevića Tara Bridge — five arches, 365 m long, 172 m above the river — was completed in 1940 after four years' work. In 1942, with Italian forces using it, the Partisans decided it had to go. The man who blew it up was Lazar Jauković, one of the engineers who had built it. He destroyed his own bridge, was captured by the Italians, and was executed on the bridge he had built and broken. There is a memorial to him at the eastern end. Very few pieces of infrastructure carry a story that complete.

On the ground

The Tara canyon is around 1,300 m deep and 80 km long. It is universally described as the deepest river canyon in Europe and the second deepest in the world after the Grand Canyon — the second claim depends on how you measure, and other canyons have competing cases, but the "deepest in Europe" part is solid.

The Tara is called Suza Evrope, the tear of Europe, for the clarity of the water, and stretches of it are drinkable straight from the river. Rafting runs out of Šćepan Polje at the confluence with the Piva, right on the Bosnian border — the classic half-day trip goes through the lower canyon. August is low water, so it is a scenic float with some lively sections rather than serious whitewater. Wetsuit provided; the water is properly cold whatever the air temperature.

Heads up

Two real ones. First: the Đurđevića Tara bridge detour from Žabljak is around 100 km of added distance, not the 25 km that circulated early in your planning. Budget for it or skip it. Second: mountain roads here are narrow, unlit, and have livestock on them. Do not plan to arrive after dark.

On the ground

The Tara raft, honestly assessed. The standard commercial run is the section from Šćepan Polje through the lower canyon — roughly 18 km, three hours on the water, grade III with a few grade IV features depending on flow. By August the water is low and warm-ish by mountain standards, which means it is more scenic float than whitewater ordeal. That is not a criticism: the point is the canyon walls, which rise over a kilometre above you, and the springs pouring straight out of the rock into the river.

The full-day option adds the upper section and a lot of transfer time on rough road. The half-day is the better value unless you specifically want the rapids. Most operators include lunch and the transfer; wetsuit, helmet and buoyancy aid are provided and non-negotiable.

Book from Žabljak or Šćepan Polje directly rather than through a coastal agency — same boats, considerably less money. confirm water levels and operating status for late August

On the ground

Walks, in ascending order of commitment.

  • Black Lake loop — 3.5 km, flat, about an hour, and genuinely lovely. Do it in the evening when the day-trippers have gone and the water goes still. Small park entry fee.
  • Ćurevac — short walk from the road to a rim viewpoint over the Tara. Access has been restricted at times for erosion. check the road and viewpoint are open
  • Ice Cave (Ledena pećina) — on the flank of Obla Glava, holding ice formations year-round. Roughly 4–5 hours return from the Sedlo road, steep, and worth it for the strangeness of finding ice in August.
  • Bobotov Kuk (2,523 m) — the big one. 6–8 hours return, some cabled sections near the top, and a real mountain day requiring an early start and a settled forecast. The usual approach is from Sedlo, which shortens it considerably.
  • Sedlo Pass by car (1,907 m) — if you do nothing else, drive this. Bare limestone, no trees, sheer walls, and almost no traffic. One of the great roads in the Balkans and nobody outside the region has heard of it.
History

Žabljak was burned repeatedly and this shapes what you see. The town has almost no old buildings, because it was destroyed in the Balkan Wars, again in the First World War, and comprehensively again in the Second — Durmitor was heavy Partisan country and the reprisals were correspondingly heavy. What you are looking at is essentially a postwar and post-1980s ski-and-hiking town, which is why it feels functional rather than picturesque.

The stćci. Scattered across this region and throughout Bosnia and Herzegovina are medieval tombstones — massive carved limestone blocks, often with strange iconography: raised hands, spirals, hunting scenes, figures dancing the kolo. There are tens of thousands of them and they date from roughly the twelfth to the sixteenth centuries. They are associated with the Bosnian Church, a distinct medieval Christian body that both Rome and Constantinople regarded as heretical, and about which remarkably little is securely known. The UNESCO listing covers sites across four countries. If you pass a field of enormous stone blocks, that is what they are.

Heads up

Altitude and weather. Žabljak at 1,456 m can drop close to freezing overnight in August and the plateau generates fast weather. If you are camping, this is the coldest night of the trip by a distance. Storms build over the massif in the afternoon exactly as they do in the Julians — the same discipline applies to Bobotov Kuk as to Slemenova špica.

Ticks. The forest and long grass here carry them. Check after walks.

Cash. Park fees, rafting, small konobas and the campsite are frequently cash. There are ATMs in Žabljak but do not arrive empty.

History

How the massif was made, and why it looks like this. Durmitor is limestone laid down under a warm shallow sea in the Mesozoic, then lifted several kilometres as Africa pushed into Europe and the Dinarides crumpled up out of the collision. The rock is the same material as the Adriatic coast you left — the difference is altitude and what came next.

What came next was ice. During the Pleistocene the plateau carried its own ice cap with glaciers running off it, and the result is the two-part landscape you are walking on: glacial forms cut into karst. The cirques, the U-shaped valleys and the lake basins are glacial. The sinkholes, the caves, the disappearing streams and the bare fluted pavement are karst. The eighteen lakes sit in glacial hollows that karst drainage has not yet emptied, which is why several of them shrink dramatically through the summer and a couple vanish altogether by September.

That is also why there is so little surface water for a place with this much rainfall — it all goes straight down. Carry what you need; you cannot count on filling a bottle from a stream.

Local knowledge

The katun system is still running. Scattered across the plateau you will pass clusters of low wooden huts with steep shingled roofs — katuni, summer shepherds' settlements. Families move livestock up from the valleys in late spring, live at altitude through the grazing season making cheese and kajmak, and come down before the snow. It is the same transhumance pattern as the Slovene planine and it has been running here for centuries.

Some katuns now sell to passers-by, and this is the best food on the plateau: kajmak (a thick soured cream skimmed and layered, somewhere between clotted cream and young cheese), skorup, fresh sir, and sometimes kačamak made on the spot. Cash, small notes. If a hut has a hand-painted sign, stop.

Also on menus in Žabljak: popara (bread, milk and cheese, breakfast), priganice, lamb ispod sača under the bell, and — if you see it — Tara trout. The local honey and the wild blueberry rakija (borovničovac) are the things to take home.

On the ground

Three lakes beyond the Black Lake, in order of effort.

  • Zminje jezero — “snake lake”, small, dark and ringed by spruce, twenty minutes off the Black Lake path and almost always empty. The reflection is better than the big lake's because there is no wind on it.
  • Jablan and Riblje — on the Sedlo road side, easy walks from parking, and the classic Durmitor postcard of still water under bare limestone.
  • Škrčka jezera — the two lakes in the Škrka cirque, deep in the massif under 1,000 m walls. A serious day out or an overnight at the mountain hut, and the most dramatic place in the park that does not require a summit.

The Ring Road (Durmitorski prsten) is the other structural thing to know: an 80 km loop road circling the massif via Sedlo, Žabljak and the Tara. It takes three to four hours with stops and it is the single best way to understand the shape of the place if the weather closes the tops.

History

Why this plateau matters to Montenegrins beyond scenery. Durmitor was central Partisan territory, and the reprisals were correspondingly severe — Žabljak was burned, villages were destroyed, and the resistance in these mountains fed directly into the postwar mythology of the republic. The war memorial in Žabljak and the scattered plaques are part of the same spomenik culture you will meet at Tjentište.

Further back, this was Drobnjaci clan country — one of the highland tribes that were, in practice, self-governing under nominal Ottoman authority. Ottoman control of the plateau was always theoretical; garrisoning a place that is under snow for six months and has no roads was never worth the cost. The clan structure, customary law and blood-feud tradition survived here later than almost anywhere in Europe.

Neđo Žižić, the guide-hero figure locals will mention, and the various vojvode commemorated on stones around the plateau, belong to that world — a society organised for permanent low-level war with an empire that never quite arrived.

Local knowledge

The Tara is drinkable, and that is the actual point. The river is classified as one of the cleanest in Europe and the water in the upper canyon is potable straight out of it — which is why the springs pouring off the walls during the raft are worth stopping under rather than just photographing.

It came very close to being dammed. In the early 2000s a proposal for a hydroelectric scheme on the Tara would have flooded a substantial part of the canyon; it produced one of the largest environmental campaigns in Montenegrin history and the parliament passed a declaration protecting the river in 2004. The canyon is a UNESCO site as part of Durmitor National Park, and the campaign is still a live reference point in Montenegrin politics.

The Đurđevića bridge is now also a zip-line — two wires strung across the canyon beside it. Short, expensive per second, and the view straight down 170 m is the product.

Sutjeska

One of the last primeval forests in Europe, and the battle that made Tito.

Heads up

This section only happens if you pick Option C on Day 13. Options A and B both route Nikšić or Podgorica → Trebinje → Mostar and skip Sutjeska entirely. If you take C, this becomes an overnight stop rather than a drive-through, and the following two sections swap order — Sarajevo comes before Mostar, not after.

History

In May and June 1943 the Germans launched Fall Schwarz — Case Black — to destroy the Partisan movement once and for all. Roughly 120,000 German, Italian, Croatian and Bulgarian troops encircled about 22,000 Partisans, including 4,000 wounded and typhus cases, in these mountains. The Partisans broke out across the Sutjeska river valley in early June. Around a third of them died. Tito himself was wounded by a bomb blast; Sava Kovačević, one of the great Partisan commanders, was killed leading the breakout.

Militarily it was a defeat with catastrophic losses. Politically it was the making of Yugoslavia. The Partisans had survived a full German encirclement; within months the Allies switched their support from the royalist Chetniks to Tito, and the postwar state was effectively decided here. Sutjeska became the founding myth — taught in every school, filmed in 1973 with Richard Burton playing Tito.

The monument at Tjentište — two enormous curved concrete wings splitting a hillside, by the sculptor Miodrag Živković, unveiled 1971 — is the most famous of the Yugoslav spomeniks. These abstract, almost science-fictional war memorials were commissioned across Yugoslavia precisely because they were abstract: no ethnic symbolism, no Orthodox or Catholic or Islamic iconography, nothing that belonged to one republic. A shared modernist language for a shared war. Many were vandalised or neglected after 1991. This one is maintained, and the memorial house behind it has frescoes of the battle.

On the ground

Sutjeska is Bosnia's oldest national park, established 1962. Within it, the Perućica reserve is one of the last two remaining primeval forests in Europe — around 1,400 hectares that have never been logged, with beech and fir over fifty metres tall and some trees more than three hundred years old. The Skakavac waterfall drops roughly 75 m inside it. Entry requires a licensed guide and is arranged through the park. book at the park office; do not plan on walking in

Maglić, 2,386 m, on the Montenegrin border, is Bosnia's highest peak. You are also crossing into Republika Srpska here — expect Cyrillic signage from Foča onwards.

Heads up

This valley is the thinnest part of the whole trip for charging, phone signal and fuel. It is also spectacularly beautiful. Both of those are because nobody lives here. Arrive with charge and downloaded maps.

Local knowledge

Perućica is the reason to come even if you do not care about the battle. It is one of the last two or three primeval forests left in Europe — around 1,400 hectares that have never been logged, with beech and fir over fifty metres tall, deadwood left where it falls, and a structure that essentially no other forest on the continent still has. Access is restricted: it is a strict reserve and you need a guide or a permit from the park office at Tjentište for the interior. The Skakavac waterfall, around 75 m, is the usual objective.

The reserve also holds brown bear, wolf, chamois and lynx. You will not see them. They are there.

Maglić (2,386 m), on the Montenegrin border, is the highest peak in Bosnia and Herzegovina and a hard but non-technical day from the Prijevor saddle — with a view over the Trnovačko lake, which is heart-shaped and photographed relentlessly for that reason.

History

On the spomeniks generally. You will meet several of these on this trip and they are worth understanding as a programme rather than as individual objects. Between the 1960s and 80s the Yugoslav state commissioned hundreds of war memorials from modernist sculptors and architects, deliberately abstract, deliberately monumental, sited at the places where things happened rather than in city squares.

The abstraction was policy. A figurative memorial has to decide whose face to carve, and in a federation of six republics and several nationalities that was the one thing it could not do. So instead: concrete wings, crystalline forms, spirals, flowers, shapes with no ethnic or religious vocabulary at all. They were pilgrimage sites for a state trying to build a shared identity out of a shared war.

After 1991 that project collapsed and many were vandalised, stripped for metal, or simply abandoned. In the last decade they have been rediscovered by photographers and architectural writers — sometimes at the cost of being treated as sci-fi curiosities detached from what they commemorate, which is worth resisting. The Tjentište monument marks a place where several thousand people died.

Heads up

If you take Option C, this is the hardest logistics day of the trip. The Šćepan Polje crossing is a small border post; the road either side is narrow and slow, and the Foča–Sarajevo stretch is mountain road rather than highway. Charging between Žabljak and Sarajevo is effectively nil — plan the leg on the assumption that you arrive on whatever you left with.

You are also crossing into Republika Srpska, one of the two entities of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Same country, same currency, different flags, different police, Cyrillic on the signage. Passports and green card out.

Mostar

A bridge named after the men who guarded it, destroyed in 1993, and rebuilt out of the same quarry.

History

The city is named for its bridge-keepers — mostari. The Stari Most was completed in 1566 for Suleiman the Magnificent by Mimar Hayruddin, a pupil of the great Ottoman architect Sinan. A single stone arch of 28.7 m, at the time among the widest in the world, springing between two towers with no central support. The legend — repeated by everyone in Mostar — is that Hayruddin was so sure it would collapse when the scaffolding came out that he arranged his own funeral and left town. It stood for 427 years.

On 9 November 1993 it was shelled into the river by Croat HVO artillery during the Croat–Bosniak phase of the war. It had no military value. Its destruction was the point: it was the physical object that proved the two communities had lived together.

The rebuild, completed in 2004, was done deliberately the hard way. Stone came from the same tenelija limestone quarry. Sixteenth-century techniques and iron cramps set in lead were used. Divers recovered original blocks from the Neretva to be reincorporated. It became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2005. Look for the stone on the bridge inscribed "Don't forget '93."

Local knowledge

The divers. Jumping off the bridge is a rite of passage that predates the tourists by centuries — the Mostar diving club claims a continuous tradition from 1664, and the annual competition each July is a serious event, now shadowed by a Red Bull cliff-diving stop. It is about 21 metres to the water and the Neretva stays brutally cold all year because it comes out of the mountains. Young men will collect money from the crowd on the bridge and then jump; if nobody pays, nobody jumps. Do not attempt it — people are injured and killed doing this, and the local divers train for years.

On the ground

The city is still divided in practice. The Bulevar and Aleksa Šantić street were the front line; today the east bank is largely Bosniak and the west largely Croat, and the city has run parallel institutions — separate utilities, separate school curricula in the same buildings, for years separate bus stations. The ruined tower block on the Bulevar, the former Ljubljanska Banka, is known as the Sniper Tower; it is a shell covered in graffiti and it is not the tourist part of town, but it is the honest part. Walking the Bulevar for ten minutes tells you more than the bridge does.

The classic view of the bridge is not from the bridge. Climb the minaret of the Koski Mehmed Pasha Mosque (narrow spiral stair, small fee) or go to its courtyard terrace. Downstream, the Kriva Ćuprija — the Crooked Bridge, 1558 — is a small humpbacked arch over a side stream that is generally understood to have been the trial run for the Stari Most.

History

Blagaj Tekija, ten minutes out of town, is a Dervish lodge built around 1520 at the foot of a 200-metre cliff, directly at the source of the Buna — one of the largest karst springs in Europe, pouring roughly 30 cubic metres a second straight out of the rock face. It was built for a Sufi order and is still in religious use. Shoes off, modest dress, and expect it to be busy. The combination — cliff, cave mouth, river being born, white Ottoman house — is the single most photographed thing in Herzegovina and deserves to be.

Počitelj, on the road south, is a fortified Ottoman hill town stacked up a limestone amphitheatre above the Neretva: the Šišman Ibrahim Pasha mosque (1563), a clock tower, a fortress, and the Gavrankapetanović house. It has hosted an artists' colony since 1964. Almost everything you see was damaged or destroyed in 1993 and has been rebuilt. Pomegranates and figs grow between the houses; people sell them at the bottom of the steps.

On the ground

Kravica is back on your list, and it is the one place on this leg where timing is everything. It is not a single fall but a wide amphitheatre of tufa cascades on the Trebižat, roughly 25 m high and 100 m across, with a pool you can swim in. Tufa is the point: the rock is still being made, precipitated out of calcium-saturated water onto moss and plant matter, so the falls are slowly building outward. It is the same process that formed Plitvice — the park you cut. In a sense you are getting the geology anyway, in a warmer place, with swimming.

In August it is heaving by mid-morning and there is a shuttle or a steep walk down from the car park. Go at opening or do not go. It pairs naturally with Počitelj in one loop south of Mostar.

Local knowledge

Herzegovinian wine is genuinely good and almost unknown outside the region. Two indigenous grapes: Žilavka (white, mineral, from the karst around Mostar and Čitluk) and Blatina (red). Blatina has a botanical oddity — it is functionally female, meaning its flowers cannot self-pollinate, so growers must interplant a pollinator variety. Every Blatina vineyard is therefore a mixed vineyard by necessity.

History

The part that is harder to say out loud. The war in Mostar came in two phases, and the second is the one the city still lives with. In 1992 Bosniaks and Croats fought together against the Yugoslav People's Army. In 1993 they turned on each other, and the front line ran down the Bulevar and Ališe Santića street — through the middle of the city, dividing it east and west. The siege of East Mostar lasted around nine months.

The bridge came down on 9 November 1993. It was not a stray round; the tower was shelled deliberately over two days. What made it a target was precisely that it had no military value — it was the object that proved the communities had been one thing.

The city was never really put back together. Mostar has, at various points since, had separate school curricula, separate bus companies, separate football loyalties, and it went sixteen years without holding a local election because the two sides could not agree an electoral system — the deadlock was only broken in 2020. Walk west from the bridge and the buildings on the old front line still carry the damage.

On the ground

Timing beats everything here. Mostar is a day-trip destination from Dubrovnik and Split, which means coach parties arrive around ten and leave around four. Before nine and after six the old town is a different place — the coppersmiths in Kujundžiluk actually working rather than selling, the bridge walkable, the light on the stone worth the trip on its own.

The Koski Mehmed Paša mosque minaret is climbable and gives the standard postcard view of the bridge from above — narrow spiral stair, small fee. The mosque courtyard terrace is free and nearly as good.

Walk over the bridge in bare feet or good shoes: the stone is deliberately ribbed and it is very slippery, especially wet.

Local knowledge

Blagaj is fifteen minutes out and, for many people, the better stop. The tekija — a Dervish lodge from around the sixteenth century — stands at the foot of a 200 m cliff where the Buna river emerges from a cave in enormous volume. It is one of the strongest karst springs in Europe. Sufi orders chose the site for the same reason it works as a photograph: water coming out of rock is legible as a symbol in every tradition in the region.

Covered shoulders and knees, shoes off inside, scarves provided. Go early; the terrace restaurants over the water fill up.

Počitelj, on the road south, is a stepped Ottoman village stacked up a hillside — a fort, a clock tower, the Šišman Ibrahim-paša mosque and a medresa, largely emptied in the war and slowly repopulated. Climb to the tower. It takes forty minutes total and almost nobody stops.

Kravica is the waterfall — a 25 m curved amphitheatre on the Trebižat with pools you can swim in. It is busy in August and there is an entry fee and a shuttle down from the car park. Go early or late; the swim is the point, not the photograph.

Local knowledge

What to eat, and the coffee ritual. Herzegovinian food is where Ottoman and Mediterranean meet:

  • Ćevapi — minced beef fingers in somun flatbread with raw onion and kajmak. Ordered by count: 5 or 10. Sarajevo and Banja Luka styles differ and people care.
  • Burek — strictly, meat only. Cheese is sirnica, spinach zeljanica, potato krompiruša. Calling them all burek marks you out instantly.
  • Japrak / dolma / sarma — stuffed leaves and vegetables. Begova čorba, bey's soup, chicken and okra.
  • Hurmastica and tufahija for dessert — syrup-soaked, and a baked apple stuffed with walnuts.
  • Herzegovina makes serious wine: Žilavka (white) and Blatina (red), both indigenous, both good, both almost unexported.

Bosnian coffee is not Turkish coffee with a different name and saying so is a small courtesy. It arrives in a džezva with a copper tray, a fildžan cup, a sugar cube and a piece of rahat lokum. The cube goes in the mouth, not the cup; you drink through it. It is meant to take half an hour. Nobody is waiting for your table.

Up the Neretva

A film set that is also a war memorial, and Tito's nuclear bunker.

History

The road north follows the Neretva into its gorge, and at Jablanica you pass a broken railway bridge lying in the river. It commemorates the Battle of the Neretva (Fall Weiss, January–March 1943), the campaign immediately before Sutjeska, in which the Partisans — burdened with thousands of wounded and typhus patients — destroyed the bridge to convince the Germans they were retreating, then repaired it enough to cross and escape south.

Here is the twist: the wreck in the river is not the original. The original was cleared after the war. In 1968 the makers of Battle of Neretva — a Yugoslav epic with Yul Brynner, Orson Welles and Sergei Bondarchuk, one of the most expensive films ever made in the country, with a poster designed by Pablo Picasso — rebuilt the bridge specifically in order to blow it up on camera. That wreck was then left in place as the memorial. The museum on the bank tells the story of the battle; the object outside is a film prop that has become the real monument.

Local knowledge

Jablanica lamb. This stretch of the M17 is lined with restaurants spit-roasting whole lambs over open fire, and it is regionally famous — Bosnians detour for it. You will smell it before you see it. Sold by weight, served with somun bread, raw onion and not much else.

History

Konjic has an Ottoman stone bridge from 1682 and, six kilometres outside town under a hillside, one of the great secrets of the Cold War: ARK D-0, Tito's atomic command bunker. Built between 1953 and 1979 at a reported cost in the billions, designed to hold the president and around 350 staff for six months after a nuclear strike, it was among the most closely held secrets in Yugoslavia — even the workers who built it did not know what it was for. When the country collapsed, the officer ordered to destroy it declined to do so.

It has since been used as a venue for a contemporary art biennial, with installations left in the corridors — the effect of walking through a 1970s command centre full of conceptual art is unlike anything else in the region. Visits are by guided tour and generally need booking in advance. book ahead if you want to go in

Konjic is also known for its woodcarving, which is on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list — a distinctive geometric style carried by a handful of local workshops for over a century.

On the ground

ARK D-0 needs booking and it is worth the trouble. Visits are by guided tour only, arranged in advance through the museum in Konjic — you cannot turn up. The tour runs around 90 minutes underground through 6,500 square metres of corridors, dormitories, conference rooms and a communications hub, all preserved with the original 1970s fittings.

The twist that makes it worth seeing rather than merely knowing about: since 2011 the bunker has hosted a contemporary art biennial, and installations by artists from across the former Yugoslavia are permanently sited in the rooms. Tito's private quarters contain conceptual art. It is a genuinely strange and effective piece of reuse. book ahead; tour times are limited and it is a working military facility

Local knowledge

Konjic woodcarving is a protected craft with a documented tradition going back to the late nineteenth century — deep-relief geometric carving on furniture and boxes, done by a handful of workshops that have passed the technique down through families. It is on the UNESCO intangible heritage list. The workshops in town will let you watch.

Jablaničko jezero, the reservoir above the dam, is where Sarajevans swim and there are small beaches along the shore. If you have hot driving behind you, this is the stop.

History

The Neretva film, in context. Bitka na Neretvi (1969) was state-financed on an enormous scale as one of a series of “Partisan westerns” — big-budget war epics with international stars, made to tell the founding story of Yugoslavia to a mass audience. The Yugoslav People's Army supplied troops and equipment as extras. It was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film.

It is a useful thing to hold in mind as you drive north: you are travelling through a landscape where the state deliberately built its own mythology into the terrain — the monuments, the museums, the films, the school trips — and where that mythology then came apart in the 1990s along exactly the lines it had been designed to paper over.

Heads up

The M17 is a single-carriageway trunk road carrying heavy freight through a gorge, with limited overtaking and a poor safety record. It is not a road to make up time on. Sections of motorway now bypass parts of it; take them where they exist. Allow more than the map suggests, particularly on a Friday.

Sarajevo

Where the twentieth century started, and very nearly where it ended.

History

Sarajevo was founded as an Ottoman city in the 1460s by Isa-beg Ishaković, who endowed a mosque, a covered market, a bathhouse and a caravanserai — the standard package for creating a city from nothing. The name comes from saray, palace. In the 1530s Gazi Husrev-beg endowed the great mosque, a madrasa, a library and the bezistan covered market that still trades. By the sixteenth century it was one of the largest cities in the Balkans, second only to Istanbul in the European Ottoman lands.

In 1878 the Austro-Hungarians took Bosnia, and they built a European capital directly onto the Ottoman one. You can stand on the exact seam. On Ferhadija street there is a marker in the pavement reading "Sarajevo, Meeting of Cultures": turn one way and you are in an Ottoman bazaar of low wooden shopfronts and copper workshops; turn the other and you are on a Habsburg boulevard of stucco apartment blocks. It is two hundred metres from the fifteenth century to the nineteenth.

Local knowledge

Within a few hundred metres of the old town you can stand at a mosque, an Orthodox cathedral, a Catholic cathedral and a synagogue. This is why Sarajevo is called the Jerusalem of Europe, and it is not a tourist-board invention — Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain in 1492 were settled here by the Ottomans and the community was substantial until 1941.

The Sarajevo Haggadah — an illuminated Hebrew manuscript made in Barcelona around 1350, which travelled with its owners through the expulsion and eventually to Bosnia — is in the National Museum. It has been saved twice: in 1942 by the museum's curator Derviš Korkut, a Muslim, who smuggled it out under his coat while a Nazi general was in the building; and again during the siege of the 1990s. Korkut and his wife also hid a young Jewish woman in their home. He is honoured at Yad Vashem.

History

Franz Ferdinand and Sophie had already survived one attempt that morning — a bomb thrown at the car, which bounced off and wounded people behind. The visit continued. Later, going to see the wounded in hospital, the motorcade turned into Franz Joseph Street by mistake; told of the error, the driver stopped to reverse, directly in front of a delicatessen where one of the failed conspirators, Gavrilo Princip, happened to be standing. He fired twice. The chain of alliances did the rest.

The corner is at the north end of the Latin Bridge, with a small museum in the building. It is an ordinary street corner. That is the striking part.

History

The siege lasted 1,425 days — 5 April 1992 to 29 February 1996 — the longest siege of a capital city in modern warfare, longer than Leningrad. Around 11,500 people were killed, including over 1,500 children. The city was surrounded on the hills you can see from every street, with no electricity, no running water and no way in or out.

Three places tell it:

  • The Tunnel of Hope (Tunel spasa) at Butmir — an 800-metre tunnel dug by hand in four months under the airport runway, the city's only link to the outside world. Food, weapons, fuel and people went through a metre-high shaft in the dark. A 25-metre section is preserved, with the house it emerged into, now a museum. It is the essential visit.
  • Sarajevo Roses — mortar craters in the pavement where people died, filled with red resin. They are scattered across the city and unmarked. Once you know what they are you cannot stop seeing them.
  • Vijećnica, the City Hall — an extraordinary Moorish-revival building from 1896 that served as the National Library. On the night of 25–26 August 1992 it was hit by incendiary shells and burned; roughly two million books and irreplaceable Ottoman-era manuscripts were destroyed. Librarians and citizens formed a human chain under sniper fire to save what they could. It reopened, restored, in 2014.

Also: the yellow Holiday Inn, built for the 1984 Winter Olympics and home to the international press corps throughout the siege, stands on the road once known as Sniper Alley. The Olympic bobsled run on Trebević is now a graffiti-covered concrete snake you can walk down through the forest, reachable by the cable car that reopened in 2018. Twelve years separate the Olympic torch from the siege lines, on the same hillsides.

Local knowledge

Ćevapi in Sarajevo are their own thing: small beef-and-lamb minced rolls, ordered in fives or tens, served inside a warm somun flatbread with raw onion and often kajmak. Baščaršija has several long-established places and locals have violent opinions about which. Any of them will be good.

Sarajevsko pivo has been brewed since 1864, and the brewery matters for a reason that has nothing to do with beer: it sits over a natural spring, and during the siege — when the water supply was cut — it became one of the city's main water points. People queued there under sniper and mortar fire to fill containers. Some were killed doing it.

For sunset, the Yellow Fortress (Žuta tabija) above the old town, an eighteenth-century bastion where locals gather. Below it spreads the Kovači cemetery — the field of slim white markers, almost all dated 1992–95. During Ramadan the cannon is fired from here to break the fast.

Try tufahija (a whole apple poached in syrup and stuffed with walnuts and cream) and boza (a thick, mildly fermented millet drink that tastes like nothing else you have had).

History

28 June 1914, and why the date matters as much as the event. Franz Ferdinand was shot on the Latin Bridge corner by Gavrilo Princip after a badly bungled morning — an earlier bomb attempt had failed, the motorcade route was changed, the driver was not told, took a wrong turn into Franz Joseph Street, and stopped to reverse directly in front of a member of the assassination team who had given up and gone for a sandwich. The sandwich detail is probably apocryphal; the wrong turn is not.

28 June is St Vitus's Day — Vidovdan — the anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo in 1389, the central date in Serbian national memory. The Archduke's visit to Sarajevo on that date, inspecting troops in a recently annexed province, was read as a deliberate provocation. It probably was not, and it did not matter.

How you describe Princip — terrorist, nationalist, freedom fighter — still maps onto political allegiance in this city. The plaque on the corner has been rewritten several times: Yugoslav-era text celebrating him, removed in the 1990s, replaced with something neutral. The museum on the corner is small and worth twenty minutes.

History

The siege was the longest of a capital city in modern history — roughly 1,425 days, from April 1992 to February 1996, longer than Stalingrad and longer than Leningrad in duration if not in death toll. Around 11,000 people were killed, including well over a thousand children. The city was encircled and shelled from the ridges you can see from any street, with an average of hundreds of shell impacts a day, and snipers covering the main boulevard — which is how Sniper Alley entered the language.

Two things to see, and they are the two that make it concrete rather than abstract:

  • The Tunnel of Hope (Tunel spasa) — an 800 m hand-dug tunnel under the airport runway, the city's only link to the outside world. Built in four months in 1993 by men digging from both ends with shovels. Food, weapons, fuel and people went through it, stooped, in water. A section is preserved as a museum near the airport. This is the single most important thing to see in Sarajevo.
  • Sarajevo Roses — shell-impact scars in the pavement, the fragmentation pattern filled with red resin, left where people were killed. They are unmarked and unsignposted. You will walk over several without noticing, and then you will start noticing.

The Vijećnica — the Habsburg-Moorish city hall that served as the National Library — was shelled with incendiaries in August 1992 and around two million books and manuscripts burned. People formed human chains under fire to save what they could. It reopened, restored, in 2014.

On the ground

One night is tight but workable if you are ruthless. A realistic shape: arrive late afternoon, walk Baščaršija and the Meeting of Cultures line in the evening light, eat ćevapi, take coffee properly. Next morning, the Tunnel of Hope early, then drive.

Baščaršija is the Ottoman bazaar quarter — the Sebilj fountain in the square, coppersmiths still hammering shell casings into coffee sets in Kazandžiluk, the Gazi Husrev-beg mosque and its bezistan. Buy the coffee set from a workshop rather than a stall.

Yellow Fortress (Žuta tabija) at sunset is the local ritual — the whole city below, the call to prayer going up from dozens of minarets simultaneously, and during Ramadan the cannon fired from here to break the fast. Free, ten-minute walk up through the old cemetery.

The 1984 Winter Olympics venues are on the mountains above — the bobsleigh track on Trebević, now derelict, graffitied and walkable, having spent the siege as a Serb artillery position. The cable car up was rebuilt in 2018. There is no more compressed image of what happened to this country than an Olympic bobsleigh run used as a gun emplacement.

Heads up

Air quality. Sarajevo sits in a bowl and suffers severe winter inversions. In August this is not an issue, but the same topography means it holds heat.

Trams and driving. The old town is pedestrianised and the surrounding streets are narrow, tram-shared and hard to park in. Use a garage.

Landmines. Bosnia still has uncleared mine-suspected areas, overwhelmingly in rural former front-line zones, marked with red-and-white signs and skull symbols. It is not a concern in cities or on marked trails, and it is an absolute concern if you wander into unmarked scrub in the hills. Stay on paths.

History

Why Bosnia became Muslim, which is the question everyone half-asks. It was not mass forced conversion. The medieval Bosnian kingdom had an unusual religious situation — a distinct Bosnian Church alongside Catholic and Orthodox communities, regarded as heretical by both Rome and Constantinople, and periodically the target of crusades and inquisitions from the Hungarian side. When the Ottomans took Bosnia in 1463 they arrived in a country whose largest religious body had no external protector and had spent two centuries being persecuted by its Christian neighbours.

Conversion then happened over roughly two centuries, driven by tax status, access to office, land tenure and urban opportunity rather than the sword. It was heavily urban — the towns Islamised far faster than the countryside — which is precisely why Sarajevo looks the way it does and why the villages around it often do not.

The consequence worth carrying: Bosniaks, Croats and Serbs in Bosnia are the same South Slavic population speaking the same language, divided by a religious boundary drawn between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries and then hardened into national identity by nineteenth-century nationalism. That is why the 1990s were a civil conflict rather than an invasion, and why the ethnic distinction is invisible to you on the street.

History

The Austro-Hungarian forty years did more to the city than you would guess. Vienna took Bosnia in 1878 under a Congress of Berlin mandate — occupying a province that legally remained Ottoman until formal annexation in 1908, which is the crisis that helped set up 1914. In four decades the Habsburgs built railways, a tram system (Sarajevo had trams before Vienna, as a test line), a brewery, schools, and an entire European quarter west of the bazaar.

They also invented an architectural style for the occasion. The Vijećnica, the National Bank, the Sarajevo of postcards — that pseudo-Moorish idiom with horseshoe arches and striped stonework is Habsburg, not Ottoman. Vienna's architects, having never built in Bosnia, borrowed from Cairo and Andalusia to design what they imagined an Islamic city should look like. Actual Ottoman Sarajevo was low, wooden, whitewashed and much plainer. You are looking at a nineteenth-century European fantasy of the East, built by the people who had just conquered it.

The Gazi Husrev-beg complex is the real thing: mosque, madrasa, hanikah, clock tower, bezistan and the Gaži Husrev-beg Library, whose manuscript collection survived the siege and now sits in a purpose-built modern building. The clock tower keeps lunar time — reset regularly so that twelve o'clock falls at sunset, for prayer timing. It is reportedly the only public clock in the world still doing this.

History

The siege, in more detail, because one paragraph does not carry it. The city was encircled from the surrounding ridges in April 1992 and stayed encircled for the better part of four years. It was not stormed — it was strangled. No electricity for long stretches, no running water, no heating through Balkan winters. People burned furniture, books and parquet flooring. They cut down every tree in the city parks. Water was collected from a handful of points under sniper observation, and queuing for it killed people.

Two incidents drove international response. The Markałe market shellings — February 1994 and August 1995 — killed dozens of civilians each, and the second directly triggered the NATO air campaign that ended the war. The market still operates; there is a memorial wall with the names.

The airport was UN-held, which is why the Tunnel of Hope had to go under the runway rather than around it. UN rules meant civilians could not cross the tarmac; the tunnel was the workaround. It was 1.6 m high in places — you moved through it bent double, often in water, with a rail for pushing carts. Around 4,000 people a day passed through at its peak, along with the city's telephone cable and a fuel pipeline.

The Siege of Sarajevo Museum and the tunnel museum are separate sites and both are worth it. The War Childhood Museum is the one people do not expect: a collection of ordinary objects donated by adults who were children during the siege, each with a short first-person text. A ballet shoe, a bicycle, a diary. It is small, quiet and by some distance the most affecting thing in the city.

Local knowledge

How the country is actually governed, in case the flags confuse you. The Dayton Agreement of 1995 stopped the war and froze its outcome into a constitution. Bosnia and Herzegovina consists of two entities — the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (Bosniak and Croat majority, subdivided into ten cantons) and Republika Srpska (Serb majority) — plus the self-governing Brčko District. There is a three-member rotating presidency with one Bosniak, one Croat and one Serb member, and an internationally appointed High Representative with powers to impose laws and remove officials.

Practically for you: crossing between entities is invisible, no checkpoint, same currency — the konvertibilna marka (KM or BAM), pegged to the euro at the old Deutsche Mark rate of roughly 1.96. But the signage switches between Latin and Cyrillic, the flags change, the police uniforms change, and the version of recent history presented in the museums changes with them. East Sarajevo, ten minutes from the centre, is in Republika Srpska.

Euros are often accepted in tourist places at a poor rate. Draw KM.

Local knowledge

A few more things worth an hour each.

  • Svežanj / the Latin Bridge — the assassination corner, and the bridge itself is Ottoman, sixteenth century, unremarkable and famous entirely by accident.
  • Baščaršija's Sebilj — the wooden fountain in the square, surrounded by pigeons. The tradition says drinking from it means you will return to Sarajevo.
  • Inat kuća — “the house of spite”, across the river from the Vijećnica. When the Habsburgs wanted the site for the city hall, the owner refused to move until they agreed to dismantle his house stone by stone and rebuild it on the far bank, plus a bag of gold. They did. It is now a restaurant and inat — stubbornness as a point of principle — is a quality Bosnians will tell you is definitional.
  • Vrelo Bosne — the source of the river Bosna at the foot of Igman, a park of springs and channels at the end of a long avenue of plane trees. Locals go on Sundays. Good if you need an hour of nothing after the museums.
  • Sarajevska pivara — the brewery, running since 1864, built over a natural spring. During the siege it was one of the few reliable sources of drinking water in the city and people queued at it under fire. The beer hall is still there.
Local knowledge

Eat, specifically. Sarajevo ćevapi are the small ones, served in fours or fives inside a somun that has been steamed over the meat juices — different from the Banja Luka style, which is flatter and joined in slabs, and people will argue about it with genuine feeling. Go to a ćevabdžinica that sells nothing else; the ones that also do pizza are not the ones.

Beyond that: begova čorba, klepe (dumplings in garlic yoghurt), dolma, sogan-dolma (stuffed onion), bosanski lonac (the layered pot stew, slow-cooked, order ahead), and ćupter or tufahija after. Boza is the fermented millet drink sold in the bazaar — an acquired taste, and worth acquiring once.

Alcohol is available everywhere but visibly less central than on the coast. Sarajevsko pivo is the beer; rakija is still offered. In more observant company, do not push it.

The road home

A city named after salt, which is where this trip began.

History

There is a closing arc here, and it is not a stretch. On Day 5 you stood on the walls of Ston — five kilometres of fourteenth-century fortification built by Ragusa to protect salt pans, because salt was the revenue that made the Republic. On Day 15 you arrive at a city whose name means salt fortress, built by prince-archbishops who got rich on exactly the same commodity, mined out of the Dürrnberg at Hallein and floated down the Salzach in barges they taxed. Two ends of Europe, four hundred years apart, both built on the one mineral that let people keep food through a winter.

The other thing to know: Salzburg was not part of Austria. For roughly a thousand years it was a sovereign ecclesiastical principality of the Holy Roman Empire, ruled by prince-archbishops who were simultaneously bishop and head of state. It only joined Austria permanently in 1816. That independence is why a city this size has a cathedral, a residence and a fortress on this scale — you are looking at the capital of a country that no longer exists.

Hohensalzburg, on the crag, was begun in 1077 and expanded for six centuries. It is one of the largest fully preserved castles in Europe and it was never taken by an enemy — though it did once hold its own former ruler: Wolf Dietrich von Raitenau, the archbishop who tore up the medieval town and rebuilt Salzburg as an Italianate baroque city, overreached politically, was deposed by his own cousin, and died a prisoner in the fortress he had inherited.

Local knowledge

The iron signs on the Getreidegasse are the detail to look up at. The street's shops advertise with elaborate wrought-iron guild signs hanging over the pavement — a boot, a pretzel, a key — because for most of the street's history most people passing could not read. The rule still binds: modern chains on that street have to make one too, which is why there is a hand-forged wrought-iron McDonald's sign.

Mozart was born at Getreidegasse 9 in 1756, and he could not wait to get out. He found Salzburg provincial, loathed working for Archbishop Colloredo, and eventually left for Vienna after a row that ended, by his own account, with a kick from the archbishop's steward. The city that now sells his face on chocolate is the one he escaped.

And the thing locals find funny: The Sound of Music means almost nothing in Salzburg. It was never a hit in Austria, most Austrians have not seen it, and the version of Austrian history in it is not one they recognise. Meanwhile busloads of visitors circle the Mirabell Gardens singing Do-Re-Mi. If you are staying at Villa Verde on Leopoldskronstraße, you are next to Schloss Leopoldskron — the lake terrace from the film — which is a rococo palace built by another prince-archbishop and later the home of Max Reinhardt, who founded the Salzburg Festival.

The Salzburg Festival will almost certainly be running on 22 August. It normally runs from mid-July to the end of August and it is the single biggest thing that happens to this city all year — which means a Saturday night in late August is peak accommodation pricing and peak scarcity. On the ground

Day 16 is about 1,000 km, Salzburg to Rotterdam, and there is no buffer day behind it — you traded it for the third Durmitor night. Two things make it more survivable than it looks:

  • It is a Sunday. Germany and Austria both ban heavy goods vehicles over 7.5 tonnes from the motorways on Sundays and public holidays, roughly midnight to 22:00. Sunday is the single best day of the week to drive that corridor — the lorry traffic that clogs the A3 and A61 simply is not there. The trade-off is more caravan and holiday traffic, and roadworks do not take Sundays off.
  • Charging stops are rest stops. Over eleven hours you need to stop anyway. Plan them where you would want to stand up regardless, leave Salzburg full, and let the car dictate the rhythm rather than fighting it.

The route retraces Day 1 in reverse for the last third — Frankfurt, Cologne, the Dutch border. If you want one last stop, Cologne cathedral is visible from the motorway and takes twenty minutes: begun in 1248, abandoned for three centuries with the medieval crane still standing on the unfinished tower, and only completed in 1880 to the original plans.

Local knowledge

If you do stop in Cologne for a beer: Kölsch comes in 0.2-litre glasses called Stangen, and the waiter — the Köbes — will keep replacing your empty with a full one, pencilling your beermat each time, without ever asking. The only way to stop him is to put your beermat on top of the glass. Nobody will tell you this.

On the ground

Sarajevo to Salzburg is the single biggest driving day of the trip — realistically 9–11 hours with stops, across three or four borders depending on the routing, and with the Bosnian and Croatian legs being the slow part. The Bosnian motorway network is still incomplete; you will be on trunk roads for a meaningful stretch before you pick up the A3 in Croatia.

Border queues in August are the variable you cannot plan around. The Bosnia–Croatia crossings can take anything from ten minutes to two hours. Croatia is in Schengen now, so the crossing into Croatia is a full external Schengen border with passport checks; Croatia into Slovenia and Slovenia into Austria are internal and usually fluid.

Vignettes: Slovenia requires an e-vignette, Austria requires one too. Both can be bought at petrol stations either side of the border with no activation delay — the ten-day wait applies only to online purchases under EU distance-selling rules, and you can waive it. Hungary would need its own if the routing changes.

Local knowledge

Salzburg in half a day, if that is all you have. The old town is compact and almost everything worth seeing is within fifteen minutes on foot:

  • Hohensalzburg — funicular up, walk down. The Golden Hall and the state rooms are the surprise; most people expect a shell and find furnishings.
  • The Residenz and the Dom — the twin seats of a prince-archbishop's double authority, facing each other across two squares. Mozart was baptised in the cathedral and later worked as court organist in this complex.
  • St Peter's Abbey and cemetery — founded around 696, one of the oldest monasteries in the German-speaking world, with rock-cut catacombs in the cliff behind. The cemetery arcades are the quietest beautiful place in the city. St Peter Stiftskulinarium next door claims a documentary mention from 803 and a decent argument for being the oldest restaurant in Europe.
  • Mirabell gardens — built by an archbishop for his mistress, with whom he had somewhere between ten and fifteen children while nominally celibate. That archbishop was Wolf Dietrich, who then died a prisoner in the fortress. The gardens are free.
  • Getreidegasse — the guild signs, and Mozart's birthplace at number 9.

Skip the Sound of Music tour unless you actively want it; the film is a much bigger deal to visitors than to Salzburgers, some of whom have never seen it.

History

The salt, properly. The Dürrnberg mines above Hallein were worked from the Iron Age — the Celtic Hallstatt culture, which gives its name to an entire archaeological period, was built on exactly this. Salt preserved in the mine has preserved the miners: bodies, clothing, tools and wooden equipment thousands of years old have come out of the workings intact.

The prince-archbishops industrialised it, controlled the Salzach traffic, taxed the barges, and used the revenue to buy the baroque city you are standing in. Mozart's Salzburg was funded by salt. So was Ragusa's Dubrovnik. The trip closes on the same commodity it opened near.

Local knowledge

Eat: Salzburger Nockerl, the enormous baked soufflé shaped as three peaks for the hills around the city — made for two people minimum, and genuinely a shared dish. Kasnöcken, cheese dumplings, if you want savoury. Bosna — a spiced sausage in a roll with curry powder and onion, invented here, and named with an irony you will now appreciate more than most tourists do.

Augustiner Bräustübl is the drink: a monastery brewery where you collect a stone mug, rinse it yourself at the fountain, and have it filled from a wooden barrel. Enormous chestnut garden, no table service, food from a row of stalls you walk past on the way in. It is the least touristy thing in the city despite being full of people.

Heads up

The last leg, Salzburg to Rotterdam, is roughly 1,000 km and 10–11 hours of driving before charging stops. Germany is the easy part of the trip for an EV — dense Ionity and Tesla coverage on the A8/A3/A61 corridor — but August Sunday and Friday traffic around Munich, Frankfurt and Cologne is the constraint, not the charging.

Austrian motorway note: some Alpine sections carry separate tolls on top of the vignette. Check the route before assuming the sticker covers everything.

And the pragmatic one: after sixteen days, the temptation is to push it in a single run. Two shorter days with a stop somewhere unremarkable in the Rhineland is a better end to a trip than arriving home wrecked.
